Abstract
With the development of information technology, information technology not only
improves human’s living environments and the quality of life but also increases the pro‑
ductivity of industries. Information technology helps businesses to provide customers with
quality services using existing resources and make the right and effective service decisions.
Accordingly, businesses have to not only understand customer needs but also pay atten‑
tion to customer emotions when they make service decisions. This study aims to build a
service recovery decision support system by adopting affective computing, artificial neural
networks and decision trees approaches. Three experiments are conducted to evaluate the
feasibility and performance of the service recovery decision support system. The experi‑
ment results show that the service recovery decision support system can have the high per‑
formance of customer recognition. Meanwhile, customer emotion can be a clue to enable
businesses to make the right service decisions in service recovery.

Keywords Affective computing · Artificial neural network · Decision support systems ·
Service recovery · Customer emotion · Decision tree


1 Introduction

The developments in information technology are changing people’s life styles. The convenience
resulting from the use of information technology has led to the trend of many services switching
to automated services. Automated services usually serve customers in a passive manner, that is,
automated services are provided only when customers execute certain commands or steps [18].
Although this type of service already satisfies most customer requirements, it does not consider
the psychological aspect of customers such as expectations and emotions [4]. During a service
delivery process, the feeling experienced by customers may affect the value of the service. If
customers are dissatisfied with a service, it implies that they had a bad impression and experi‑
ence owing to a poor service design or customer interaction process [48, 49].

   Service interaction design is an emerging academic field, which emphasizes on the com‑
bination of customer experience and service design, in order to provide services that can
satisfy customer requirements in a fast and effective manner [22]. Customer involvement
is an important element in service interaction design [18]. In general, among the many
methods in which people can express their requirements during a service delivery process,
voice dialogs are the most direct method of interaction. Owing to the rapid development
of information technology, many voice interaction services have become popular, and they
attempt to improve customer experience [54]. For example, using Siri, which is a voice
recognition-based personal assistant service for iPhone, customers can acquire personal‑
ized services (such as navigation or trip planning) through voice control. This innovative
service also allows multiple people to stay in touch through interaction using information
technology [5].
   Currently, the service industry is a key driver for major economic activities in many
countries, with increased competition in the service environment. Besides customer
requirements, effectively retaining customers is an important issue for businesses. A cus‑
tomers’ emotion is an important factor that influences customer satisfaction and loyalty [9].
The interactive service between the business and customers should not only learn customer
needs, but also evaluate the emotions expressed by customers through conversations. After
receiving the service, the customer’s emotions reflect the level of satisfaction. Therefore, if
the emotional factor is integrated into the development of service policies, it will help the
business to provide appropriate services to their customers.
   Picard [39] introduced the term “affective computing,” in which sensors are used in
information technology to measure the physiological signals of customers, such as the
changes in facial expression or heart rate. In addition, these signals are used to understand
the customer’s emotion in order to provide a suitable response. Affective computing not
only studies whether an information technology can determine customers’ emotion, but
also embeds emotional elements into information technology, so that decisions can be
made based on the customers’ perspective [40, 41, 53]. Appropriate services are provided
based on the different emotions, which change the conventional information technology
decision-making method.
   People and information technologies can interact through many different methods. Cur‑
rently, the most common interaction method is verbal communication, which achieves
communication through the characteristics of sound waves and the content of messages
[25]. However, information technology is ineffective at understanding human feelings.
Although people can interact with information technology using the same communication
method, information technology cannot comprehend emotional communication. In order
for information technology to understand the emotional response of customers through
interactive conversations, Picard evaluated user emotion through voice signals by analyz‑
ing voice signal characteristics and categorizing emotions [40, 41].
   Petrushin [38] applied voice emotion recognition for commercial activities and estab‑
lished customer emotion recognition systems in customer service centers, which require
the customer service staff to communicate with the customers to evaluate their emotion and
provide appropriate services. Li et al. [30] considered the traditional e-learning model to
be ineffective as it lacks interaction and reduces the users’ willingness to learn, and there‑
fore, he proposed a voice interactive learning model. It allows the users to respond to the
learning situation at any time, so that the system can recognize the users’ emotion through
voice signals and provide timely encouragement and suggestions in order to increase the
users’ willingness to learn and improve the effectiveness of the e-learning model. From
these examples, it is clear that the concept of affective computing applications has become

2 Literature review

2.1 Service recovery

According to Vargo and Lusch [52], the definition of a service is the application of com‑
petency for the benefit of other parties. When service providers provide customers with
high-quality services to meet customer expectation, customers have high satisfaction that
can increase their loyalty for the services and service providers. However, there are many
critical factors influencing the success of the service delivery including service quality, ser‑
vice operation, frontline employee, customer expectation and so on [37, 46, 54]. While ser‑
vice provides make mistakes during service delivery that cannot appropriately fulfill cus‑
tomer needs, service failures immediately occur. Service failures directly affect customers
to perceive negative experiences and cause customers who could be not willing to employ
perceived failure services next time [36]. The business reputation and benefits of service
providers could become more and more negative if service providers cannot instantly deal
with service failures. Consequently, how to recover the failures, that is service recovery,
for service providers and researchers has been extremely important issue within the service
field.
    Service recovery is defined that service providers employ recovery actions to respond to
service failures [18]. That is, service recovery is a process to return dissatisfied customers
to achieve a state of satisfaction with a service provider after a service has failed to meet
customer expectations. According to Craighead et al. [9], customers will certainly achieve
high satisfaction after service providers offer real-time and quality remedial services
in comparison with the appropriate service delivery to fulfill customers. It is extremely
important and necessary for service providers to define suitable service recovery strategies
and operations and also provide real-time and effective remedial services with customers.
To our knowledge, although the issue of service failure and service recovery has been stud‑
ied by many academic fields including marketing field, service management field, business
administration field, operation management field, etc. [10], these pioneer research only
proposed lots of contributions by using empirical approaches (that is, the questionnaire

survey and the statistic method). Especially for the consideration of real-time and effective
remedial services, there is less research investigating the problem based on the perspective
of system development.
   Furthermore, service recovery can be regarded as a complex process. The success of
service recovery can be influenced by the capabilities of service providers, the nature of
services, the characteristics of customers, the guidelines of recovery strategies and poli‑
cies [19, 26]. It is a critical challenge for service providers to immediately make responses
to service failures from customers because service providers have to take aforementioned
factors into account at the same time. The main roles to execute remedial services are the
frontline employees and middle managers who are all human beings [49]. The frontline
employees and middle managers usually struggle to make the right decision for implement‑
ing remedial services within the limited time since they need to be based on their past
experiences and domain knowledge.
   Besides, when customers perceived service failures, they usually have negative emotions
for service providers. Smith and Bolton [48] noted that customers’ emotional responses to
service failures can affect their recovery effort evaluations and satisfaction judgments dur‑
ing service recovery. It is useful to understand how customers felt during past service fail‑
ure and recovery encounters influence subsequent evaluations of service encounters [27,
33]. Customer emotions have important practical implications what customers feel about
a product or service impacts on customer satisfaction, repeat purchase, switching, negative
word of mouth, complaining to third parties and loyalty [11]. Undoubtedly, customer emo‑
tion can be a useful clue for service providers to the implementation of successful service
recovery.
   Customers’ feelings and perceptions are the most critical factors to measure if service
providers offer appropriate remedial services to deal with service failures. Hence, customer
emotions usually directly reflect their feelings of received remedial services [32]. To our
knowledge, although many research emphasized the importance of understanding customer
emotions, there is less research applying customer emotions to a service recovery system
decision support system in real time. If service providers accurately gather the data of cus‑
tomer emotions to generate the useful information for service recovery, the information
can help service providers to understand customer emotions after perceiving remedial ser‑
vices. Meanwhile, service providers can predict customer behaviors by analyzing emotions
during service recovery. Accordingly, a systematical and effective mechanism for service
recovery should be necessary for service providers to implement remedial services in real
time. A decision support system is an applicable system in service recovery.


2.2 Affective computing

Picard [39] proposed the idea of affective computing that can recognize human emotions to
apply into information systems. The intelligent information system can effectively under‑
stand human emotions to offer appropriate interactive activities in real time [45]. Further‑
more, the intelligent information system not only can imitate real-world human behaviors
by employing the idea of affective computing but also can help decision-makers to accu‑
rately make the right choice [12, 53]. There are four main indicators of affective comput‑
ing which includes recognizing emotions, expressing emotions, having emotions and emo‑
tional intelligence [39–41] as delineated in Fig. 1.
   The purpose of recognizing emotions is to understand what emotions humans could convey
and explore the main cause and environmental situations of bringing their emotions [4]. The

Figure 2 shows the design logic of the conceptual framework of the service recovery decision

3.2 Acoustical preprocessing

The overall architecture of the service delivery mechanism in this study can be divided
into the front-end and back-end system. The front-end system serves as the interactive
interface for the voice and text communication between the customers and the informa‑
tion technology. Voice messages are also collected by the front-end system. The back-end
performs voice emotion recognition processing on the received voice messages. In order to
effectively analyze the voice messages, this study determines emotion characteristic values
embedded in the voice messages though voice signal processing, using emotion recogni‑
tion characteristic parameters such as pitch, energy, formant, and Mel-frequency cepstral
coefficient (MFCC), and it identifies values that can be categorized as positive and negative
emotions [23].
   Speech is one of the methods used by people for communication. Through the under‑
standing of semantics and observation of changes in intonation, people can express
their needs and emotion changes. However, for information technology, it is difficult to

for emotion recognition, voice signals need to be processed into signals that can be recog‑

3.3 Backpropagation neural network

In order to perform emotion categorization for the processed voice signal, this study
employs a neural network with a good application range and flexibility. However, there
exist different types of neural networks. To effectively analyze the input data and obtain
the expected results, a backpropagation neural network algorithm is selected, which uses
supervised learning that can make multiple corrections to the output. This enables more
accurate emotion categorization results for the voice messages through the calculation of
the backpropagation neural network.
   The main framework of the backpropagation neural network includes three layers: input,
hidden and output, with error learning ability. The neural network can calculate the error
between the customers’ expected output and actual output; adjust the output value of the
neural network; and improve the output results to match customer expectation through a
continuous correction process [56]. Such a learning process also makes the neural network
more flexible about error toleration. Even if customers input a new question with different
emotional characteristics, the neural network can provide similar results based on similar
conditions, which helps to widen the application of the proposed method.
   The input layer is only used for receiving the characteristic parameters and does not
have any calculation capability. Xi in Fig. 4 is the input characteristic parameter. The input
data of this study are the voice characteristic parameter obtained from voice signal process‑
ing. After the input values enter the input layer, the neural network transmits them to the
hidden layer and calculates the output values. Hj in the figure is the output value. Accord‑
ing to the neuron output equation, the hidden layer output equation is similar to that of the
output layer. The neuron output value is obtained by adding the output value from the pre‑
vious layer and the weighted value W and subtracting the bias (b) from the error learning

3.4 C4.5 decision tree algorithm

In order to better explain the relationship between emotions and services, a decision tree is
used in this study as a method of service delivery [50]. The nodes and tree diagram of the
decision tree are established based on the characteristics of the collected data, which can
not only identify the most influential characteristics but also directly explain the relation‑
ship for selecting that service. There are different types of decision trees. The C4.5 deci‑
sion tree algorithm is used in this study as the service classification methodology [42, 43].
It is capable of dealing with both continuous and discrete types of data and establishing
multi-node trees, which enables the proposed mechanism to generate more diversified and
more appropriate services for the customers.
    Decision trees can describe classifications and make decisions based on the relation‑
ships between the characteristics and results [44]. Therefore, in order to increase the ana‑
lytical ability of the decision tree, it is important to identify appropriate characteristics from
the collected data and use them as the categorization node. The C4.5 algorithm is used in
this study as the algorithm for decision trees [42, 43]. C4.5 is a new methodology from the
improved ID3. Its characteristic selection criteria are also based on the correction of infor‑
mation gain selected by ID3, and it uses the gain ratio as the characteristic selection criteria.
This section illustrates the formula used for calculating the gain ratio in C4.5 [42, 43].

calculated error ratio, in order to avoid the errors during the evaluation of error ratio as a
result of being over optimistic, which reduces the accuracy of the decision result.


4 Experiment validation and results

This study mainly focuses on the design of an emotion recognition-based service recovery
decision support system and tests whether this system is capable of resolving the research
problems using experiments. Three experiments are designed in this study, including the
establishment of emotion classification architecture, the setup of service recovery decision-
making model and the evaluation of system effectiveness. This section describes the three
experiments in detail and concludes with the discussion of the experimental results.

4.1 Experiment 1: the establishment of emotion classification architecture

4.1.1 Experimental objective

This experiment establishes an effective emotion classification architecture using the
backpropagation neural network. Setting up a number of hidden layer neurons in the
neural network is an important topic, which usually needs to be decided based on the
complexity of the problem. Since the number of hidden layer neurons in the neural net‑
work affects the effectiveness of the neural network training, more hidden layer neurons
imply higher accuracy; however, they also need longer training time. On the contrary,

2236                                                                        Y.-H. Hsieh, S.-C. Chen


considerably many neurons may result in the overfitting of the neural network, which
leads to the over-training of the network and less satisfactory classifications and predic‑
tions. A trial-and-error method is usually used to decide the required number of hidden
layer neurons. It runs the test sequentially to identify the most appropriate number of
hidden layer neurons.


4.1.2 Experimental design

This study is based on the research of Burkhardt et al. [3] and chooses to use the Ber‑
lin emotional speech database. Each voice datum in this database has already been defi‑
nitely classified based on the emotion. Dube and Menon [14] stated that a positive emotion
includes happiness and joy, and the negative emotion includes anger and sadness. Anger
is defined as a negative emotion, and happiness is defined as a positive emotion in order
to recognize customer emotions after service failure or service recovery. In all relevant
experiments of this study, angry reactions are uniformly represented by the negative emo‑
tion and happy reactions are uniformly indicated by the positive emotion. Furthermore,
Garson [16] and Kavzoglu and Mather [24] proposed one feasible rule to determinate the
optimal number of training sizes that should be at least 10 times the number of inputs. This
study selects 199 voice data (including angry and happy voices) from the Berlin emotional
speech database as samples for emotion classification. The number of training sizes can
fulfill this criterion. A part of the sample data can be used as training data, and the remain‑
ing can be used as test data. 80/20 is a commonly used rule for the division of training and
test data and is also used in this study. These voice data samples were selected at random
and divided into 134 training data and 65 test data. In addition, Coleman et al. [8] indicated
that the simulation results easily converge when the number of simulations is 1000; there‑
fore, we perform 1000 simulations in this study.
    MFCC is regarded as appropriate feature to be used in emotion recognition with high
recognition rate, simple calculation, better ability of distinction and high robustness to
noise [6, 34, 35, 51]. Consequently, this system uses the processed MFCC before the voice
signal is used as the input data for the neural network input layer. As a result, the number of
nodes on the input layer is set to be the same as the dimension of the MFCC, which is 12.
As stated above, when customers encounter service failure, they generate negative and pos‑
itive emotions. Thus, two nodes are set up for the output layer. According to Yao [56] and
Leon et al. [29], most problems can be solved by using a single hidden layer. The network
could fail to converge on an acceptable solution when multiple hidden layers are adopted.
To avoid overcomplicating the network architecture and reducing the training effectiveness,
a single hidden layer is used to achieve the emotion classification result. Simultaneously,
the number of neurons in the hidden layer is decided by the following formula:

5 Discussion

The aim of this study is to recognize human emotion using information technology, and
based on the recognition formulate appropriate decisions. This study uses backpropaga‑
tion neural network as the emotion classification method. Results from experiment 3 have
73.57% recognition rate for positive emotion and 72.14% for negative emotion. From the
experiment results, it is evident that the backpropagation neural network is effective in
emotion classification and it enhances the effectiveness of emotion application in service
decision-making. According to Douglas-Cowie et al. [13], voice signal characteristics are
similar in emotion expression, and a significant error recognition rate is unlikely in voice
emotion recognition because of the change in language. That is, even with a language bar‑
rier, change in emotion can still be recognized by the tone, intensity, and pitch of the voice,
which is also supported by the positive emotion recognition rate in experiments 1 and 3.
   The neural network established on the German voice emotion database achieved 89.08%
recognition rate accuracy in experiment 1 and only 72.14% recognition rate accuracy in the
operation of the actual system in experiment 3. One possible reason is that all the subjects
were in a simulated experiment and did not really encounter service failure. As a result,
the emotions expressed were different from when service failure actually occurs, and this
consequently affected the recognition accuracy. In restaurants, if service failure occurs, the
customers naturally express their emotions based on the degree of service failure. How‑
ever, in experiment 3, the customers were required imagine the simulated service failure
and provide a suitable emotional response to that situation. For example, when a subject
expressed a complaint in a gentle demeanor, there is a likelihood that the system may rec‑
ognize it as a positive emotion, which results in a recognition error. In addition, because
subjects perform oral emotional response recordings alone with a computer, i.e., when
there is no actual external stimulation, the subjects may easily become shy or overwhelmed


13
A decision support system for service recovery in affective…                                2247


during the process or may even be intrigued by the experiment, resulting in some inaccu‑
rate expression of emotion.
   Furthermore, according to the survey results in experiment 2, respondents have differ‑
ent levels of importance of four service failure situations. They also expected restaurants
to provide suitable measures to deal with the specific service failures. Table 9 shows that
respondents paid attention to situation 2 (the percentage of probably important and defi‑
nitely important is 70%) and situation 3 (the percentage of probably important and defi‑

   However, according to the experiment results of experiment 3, the service recovery

   Consequently, customer expectations of service recovery measures are influenced by

conditions of customers and businesses to develop a faultless service recovery decision
support system. Values will be created via delivering appropriate service recovery meas‑
ures when service failures occur.


5.1 Managerial implication

Under service failure situations, the emotion of the customers can be used not only to eval-
uate the effectiveness of services provided by the business, but also to provide evidence and
act as a reference for service decision-making. Currently, the commonly used methods for
businesses to collect feedback from customers are through questionnaires or verbal conver‑
sations. The questionnaires mainly include satisfactory level and open-end-related ques‑
tions for customers to complete, which is relatively more complicated and needs to be man‑
ually verified in order to provide service recovery measures. Although verbal conversation
can yield timely customer feedback, not all customers will clearly point out the problems of
a business or proactively ask for expected service. Both methods have their pros and cons.
According to de Witt and Brady [11], service failures adversely affect customer evaluations
of a business, which tends to be reflected through customer emotions. Service recovery
measures by a business may be capable of retrieving lost credit rating and improving the
customers’ emotions, which turns service failures to opportunities of improvement for the
business. Therefore, this study considers emotion as a sufficient parameter for business ser‑
vice decision-making and, consequently, for evaluating the influence of service decisions
on customers.
   Businesses can use the voice emotion recognition method by simply detecting voice
characteristics to identify customer emotions and determine service decisions without vio-
lating customer privacy. Based on the affective computing concept proposed by Picard
[39], this study applies the concept of emotional intelligence into human–computer interac‑

6 Conclusions

6.1 Academic contributions

Combining affective computing with service science, applying emotion in service research
areas and using emotion to create greater service value. Emotions can be effectively used
in human–computer interaction to create more efficient services. This study shows that
emotions can be reflective of customer dissatisfaction toward services. Businesses can

                                                                                  13
2250                                                                       Y.-H. Hsieh, S.-C. Chen


use the service recovery decision support system to recognize customers’ emotions and
provide effective service recovery measures. Besides voice characteristics, this study also
accounts for personal factors of customers and service failure situations in the decision-
making process, in order to find the relationship between the emotion expressed and the
environment, thereby enabling the service recovery decision support system to more accu‑
rately provide appropriate service to the customers. This study mainly focuses on exploring
the effectiveness of emotions in service decision-making and the application of emotion
recognition and emotion intelligence. To our knowledge, current research results have yet
to specifically propose any service recovery decision support system based on affective
computing. In addition, because of the high technical requirement for emotion recogni‑
tion, its application in business environments is limited owing to inflexibility. According
to the experimental results of this study, the service recovery decision support system does
not only embed the concept of service interaction design, but also enables application of
emotion in the service area, which provides businesses with a faster and more convenient
service decision-making method.
    Hybrid methods (i.e., artificial neural networks and decision trees) are adopted to
build the service recovery decision support system that can be regarded as a fundamen-
tal research groundwork. Previous research paid attention to the performance of emotion
recognition and the improvement in artificial neural networks. However, to our knowledge,
there is less research applying the idea of affective computing and emotion recognition into
the service industry. This study not only analyzes the principle of artificial neural networks
for affective computing but also employs the logic of decision trees for service recovery
decision strategies. Applying hybrid methods to build the service recovery decision sup‑
port system can be regarded as an important foundation, especially in service contexts.
Hence, this study enables businesses to effectively and efficiently examine the possibility
of service failures and respond to customers’ complaints via emotion recognition. Mean‑
while, this study also can be considered as an essential foundation for further researchers to
take other critical concerns into account for better emotion recognition in affective comput‑
ing and service recovery decision support systems.
    Applying emotion in the service delivery process using service interaction design, estab-
lishing a service recovery decision support system and verifying its effectiveness using ser-
vice recovery situations. This study established a customer-focused service recovery deci‑
sion support system using service interaction design. This study uses service recovery as
the research context to verify the effectiveness of the service recovery decision support sys‑
tem. According to Picard [40], not everyone can explicitly express their emotion owing to
different personalities and backgrounds. Thus, there are different complaint behaviors and
expectations for recovery measures. Although businesses can use questionnaires or inter‑
views to collect information about service failures, according to Wirtz and Mattila [55],
customers expect fast and effective service recovery measures at the time of service fail‑
ure; to provide the same through means of questionnaires and interviews requires a large
amount of time and labor, which may not necessarily recover customer satisfaction. There‑
fore, this study tries to use different service delivery methods to achieve more efficient and
effective service recovery. Experimental results show that the expected service recovery
measure is not only affected by the service failure situation or personal background. Appro‑
priate service recovery decisions can also be made from context-related attribute informa‑
tion. Businesses can also find more factors that can influence service recovery decision-
making from service interaction design. Businesses should not be limited by factors such
as customer background or emotion, but should provide more diversified service recovery
decision-making based on broader conditions to improve customer satisfaction on services.

6.2 Practical contributions

Businesses can apply and extend the mechanism and architecture of the service recovery
decision support system provided in this study, set up unique and effective service decision-
making methods and provide high-quality service measures to customers. Besides the abil‑
ity of recognizing customer emotions, this service recovery decision support system can
also find the correlation between emotion and decisions and explore the most appropriate
service recovery measures to the customers, based on customers’ backgrounds and service
failure situations. It is even capable of assisting front-end staffs to evaluate service fail‑
ure situations and provide timely and effective service recovery measures. It reduces labor
costs and helps in service recovery decision-making. In addition, this study includes cus‑
tomers’ emotion in the service recovery decision support system to help businesses decide
what service recovery measures to provide when service failures occur. Even if customers
do not specify the expected service, high-quality service can still be delivered to custom‑
ers using this service recovery decision support system. Not only can the mechanism and
architecture of this service recovery decision support system be used in service recovery,
but it also can be widely used in other service situations. Businesses just need to establish
appropriate service decision-making rules based on emotion recognition. It can then carry
out the service decision, and the appropriate service measures can be proactively provided
during the interaction process to increase customer satisfaction and loyalty to the business.
    Businesses can follow the method used in this study to set up more realistic ser-
vice recovery decision-making rules based on different service situations and customer
emotions. Customer emotions have influence on business decision-making. By ana‑
lyzing changes in customer emotion, businesses can clearly understand if the service
delivered is effective and improve their service delivery process and content. In reality,
businesses analyze customer background information before providing appropriate ser‑
vices, but customers’ emotion is relatively less used in service decision-making. This
study finds that emotion can not only represent customer satisfaction to the services,
but can also be used as evidence for service decision-making. According to the service
recovery decision-making rules in this study, businesses can classify customers based
on their background and also find new service decision-making rules from dynamic
characteristics, such as the frequency of eating out weekly, so as to find potential cus‑
tomer groups. Then, depending on different types of customers, more diversified and
appropriate services can be provided.
    Businesses can effectively and actively identify customers with service failures via
emotion recognition in real-time service contexts. It is extremely crucial for businesses
to understand the consequences of customers’ complaints and detect service failures
in practice. Conducting customer satisfaction surveys to collect complaints has been
common way after services are completed. However, service failures could occur dur‑
ing service delivery. It is difficult for businesses to immediately provide customers
with appropriate recovery strategies and tactics in response to service failures and cus‑
tomer complaints. The effectiveness of service recovery directly influences businesses
to maintain customer satisfaction. Hence, this study aims to provide businesses with an
efficient way to deal with service failures in real time by proposing the service recov‑
ery decision support system. Real-time recovery responses to customers’ complaints
and service failures handily lead to the service recovery paradox that make customers
feel well treated and satisfied.

6.3 Limitation and future research

This study only classifies emotions as positive emotion and negative emotion. Previous stud‑
ies showed that emotions can be classified as general emotion, positive emotion and nega‑
tive emotion. de Witt and Brady [11] found that negative emotion may occur more frequently
before the customers encounter service failure. This study uses restaurant service as the exper‑
imental situation with the main consideration that customers generate negative emotion when
there is service failure and positive emotion after receiving service recovery measures. Thus,
this study does not consider the situation of general emotions. However, emotions can be clas‑
sified in more detailed groups based on the ways of expression or the different degrees of feel‑
ings, and not only by different types but also by the different degrees of emotions. Therefore,
future researchers can try to include greater variety of emotions into consideration and provide
a greater scope for discussion based on different emotional statuses. Besides, customers have
different levels of perception and expectation of recovery measures according to their back‑
grounds, cultures, educations, past experiences and so on. Some customers may think multiple
measures should be provided by businesses since they would have high expectation to receive
munificent compensations. Researcher could take the notions into account to enrich the deci‑
sion rules of the service recovery decision support system in the future. In addition, this study
only selected voice signals as the method to recognize emotion. Future studies may combine
it with other emotion recognition methods and apply other emotion classification methods, in
order to obtain more accurate emotion recognition results.
