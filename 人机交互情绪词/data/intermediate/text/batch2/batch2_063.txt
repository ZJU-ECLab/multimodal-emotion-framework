Abstract
   The present study investigated the psychophysiological effects of positive and negative affective
interventions in human– computer interaction during and after the interventions. Eighteen subjects
were exposed to pre-programmed mouse delays in an interactive problem-solving task. Following
the mouse delays three types of conditions were used: positive or negative interventions given via
speech synthesizer, and no intervention. Facial electromyographic responses were recorded from the
zygomaticus major and corrugator supercilii muscle sites. These muscles control smiling and
frowning, respectively. Smiling activity was significantly higher during the positive than the other
conditions. It was also significantly higher after the positive interventions than the no intervention
condition. The frowning activity attenuated significantly more after the positive interventions than
the no intervention condition. Following the positive interventions the users’ problem solving
performance was significantly better than after no intervention. In all, the results suggest that both
types of affective intervention had beneficial effects over ignoring the user. The results suggest
further that positive intervention may be especially useful.

1. Introduction

    The field of human –computer interaction has recently been widened by a new research
field of affective computing. Picard (1997) defined affective computing as computing that

relates to, arises from, or deliberately influences emotions. The growing interest in
affective computing originates from findings in psychology and psychophysiology of
human emotions, which highlight the importance of emotion in human behavior. For
example, there is evidence that affective processing influences cognitive processing and
may even have primacy over it (Murphy and Zajonc, 1993). In addition, there is substantial
evidence that affects play a central role in human-human communication (Surakka and
Hietanen, 1998; Zajonc, 1980). With respect to human –computer interaction, it was found
very recently that positive affective feedback significantly facilitated recovery from
physiological arousal and accentuated cognitive operations (Aula and Surakka, 2002). In
sum, it seems that emotions could be of great importance in human –computer interaction.
   Emotion can be defined as discrete categories or as a space of different dimensions. A
well-known discrete emotions framework is the argument for basic emotions by Ekman
(1993). He defined six basic emotions: anger, disgust, fear, joy, sadness, and surprise. On
the other hand, according to the dimensional emotions framework, emotion can be defined
as a set of dimensions, which together define the emotional space (Bradley and Lang,
1994, 2000). Out of the original three dimensions (valence, arousal, and dominance),
valence and arousal are the two most commonly used dimensions. The valence dimension
varies from negative to positive emotional pole and the middle of the dimension represents
neutral affect. In the same fashion, the arousal dimension varies from calm to highly
aroused pole. Lang and his co-workers have suggested that the valence dimension reflects
the presence of an appetitive or aversive motivation system of the human brain. This
system is linked to behavioral tendencies of approach and withdrawal, respectively. The
arousal dimension has been suggested to reflect the intensity of either appetitive or
aversive systems (Bradley and Lang, 2000; Lang, 1995; Lang et al., 1993).
   Many studies have shown that various changes in physiological activity are integrally
related to emotional responses. For example, there is evidence that facial muscle activity is
related to emotions. It has been shown that the electrical activity of zygomaticus major (the
muscle that draws the lip corners up producing a smile) is positively associated with
subjectively experienced valence, whereas the electrical activity of corrugator supercilii
(the muscle that knits and lowers the brows) is negatively associated with experienced
valence (Bradley and Lang, 2000; Cacioppo et al., 1986; Dimberg, 1990). Thus, by
measuring the activations of these muscles it is possible to obtain information about the
subject’s affective state. In current emotion research, it is in most cases imperative to
monitor human physiology while studying emotions (Öhman and Birbaumer, 1993).
   In human– computer interaction research, the use of emotion-related physiological
signals has been infrequent. Scheirer et al. (2002) used pattern recognition to recognize
user frustration from two physiological signals: galvanic skin response and blood volume
pressure. Their results showed recognition rates of user frustration that were significantly
better than random. Other proposed physiological measures have included, for example,
pupil size measurement (Partala et al., 2000; Partala and Surakka, 2003) and the
measurement of electrical activity of facial muscles with electromyography (EMG)
(Laakso et al., 2001; Partala et al., 2001).
   There are different ways in which emotions could be utilized in human – computer
interaction. One approach has been the study of alleviating the negative emotions related
to the use of information technology. Even though there have been advances in software

and user interface design, problem situations are still common, and they often have
negative effects on the user’s affective state. Suggested negative consequences may
include, for example, increased stress, decreased productivity, and decreased trust towards
computers from the side of the user (Klein et al., 2002).
    It seems possible to influence the user’s negative emotional state, and reduce the related
negative consequences. Klein et al. (2002) conducted an experiment in which the user was
deliberately frustrated during a game-playing situation, and the user was given affective
support using a text-based agent. Their results showed that the users chose to interact
significantly longer with the computer when they were given textual affective support by
the agent as compared to the condition in which no support was given. The use of textual
affective support also led to significantly longer computer usage as compared to an
opportunity to vent feelings using a text box. Based on these findings Klein et al. (2002)
suggested that ‘computers are capable of alleviating strong, negative emotions, even when
they are the source of these negative emotions’.
    There are several possible techniques for the emotional stimulation of the user. In
addition to textual or other visual emotional messages, auditory stimulation offers a
promising alternative. There is evidence that different emotional reactions can be evoked
using different auditory stimuli. In addition to the prosodic features of auditory messages,
the use of speech contents as such seems especially suitable for emotional stimulation. It is
known that people recognize emotions from both the emotion-related prosodic cues and
the contents of spoken messages (Scherer et al., 1991). The emotion-related prosodic cues
in speech include, for example, fundamental frequency, pitch range, intensity, articulation,
and speech rate (Cahn, 1990; Murray and Arnott, 1993; Scherer et al., 1991). There is
evidence that both types of emotional cues (i.e. verbal content and prosodic features) can
be used to evoke emotional reactions (Scherer et al., 1984).
    We have recently found evidence that emotionally provocative sounds can be used to
evoke different autonomic nervous system responses and subjective experiences (Partala
et al., 2000; Partala and Surakka, 2003). There is also evidence that careful manipulations
of the prosodic cues of vocal affect expressions result in differential responses in the
electromyographic activity of emotion-related facial muscles and behavioral approach-
withdrawal responses (Hietanen et al., 1998).
    Importantly, it has been shown by Aula and Surakka (2002) that it is possible to
influence the user’s emotions by using different affective wordings in the messages given
using synthetic speech, while keeping the speech prosody constant. In their experiment,
the use of emotionally negative, neutral, and positive synthetic speech feedback to
cognitive tasks resulted in significant differences in the activity of the autonomic nervous
system and enhanced response times while solving cognitive problems. The subjective
ratings showed that the subjects also experienced the emotional feedback as negative,
neutral or positive.
    The present aim was to study the effects of affective interventions using synthetic
speech with emotional content. The interventions were given when the subjects’ problem
solving had been interrupted by mouse delays. Facial EMG responses were measured
during and after positive and negative worded affective interventions. Task performance in
problem solving was analyzed following these interventions. Facial EMG activity and task
performance were also measured in relation to a no intervention condition. This condition

2. Methods


2.1. Subjects

   Twenty-four volunteer students participated in the experiment Data from six subjects
were discarded due to technical problems or excessive eye blinking Thus, the results are
based on data from 18 subjects (nine females, mean age 24.2 years) All the subjects had
normal or corrected-to-normal vision, normal color vision, and normal hearing by their
own report.

2.2. Equipment

   The experiment was run on a Pentium II PC computer under the Windows NT operating
system. A 1500 Nokia 500 Xa monitor in the 1024 £ 768 resolution mode was used as a
display. The EMG activity was recorded with a Grassw Model15 RXie 8-channel
differential amplifier and PolyVIEWe PRO/32 2.0 software. For a bipolar EMG
recording, In Vivo Metric Ag/AgCl (E220X) surface electrodes filled with electrode paste
were used. Timing markers were sent from the stimulus computer to the EMG recording
device with a special cable using parallel port communications.

2.3. EMG recordings

   For the EMG recording, the electrodes were attached according to the guidelines by
Fridlund and Cacioppo (1986). The subject’s skin was cleaned and slightly abraded with
electrode paste. Electrodes were attached on the left side of the face above the zygomaticus
major muscle site (the muscle that draws the lip corners up producing a smile) and
corrugator supercilii muscle site (the muscle that knits and lowers the brows producing a
frown). The EMG signal was amplified with high-pass and low-pass filters set at 100 and
1000 Hz, respectively. The sampling rate was 1 kHz. The signal was amplified 20,000
times for zygomaticus major and 50,000 times for corrugator supercilii. Eye movements
and eye blinks were monitored with electrodes attached above and below the subject’s
right eye. This signal was amplified 2000 times with a high-pass filter set at 0.3 Hz and a
low-pass filter set at 30 Hz. The interelectrode impedances were , 10 kV. The signals
were also monitored in real time on a computer display during the whole experiment.

2.4. Tasks

   The experimental tasks consisted of 12 problem-solving tasks, in which the subject had
to arrange colored squares into a given order as shown with indicator squares (Fig. 1). In
each task, there were 16 big gray squares with small colored indicator squares by their
side. There were also two big auxiliary squares at the center of the screen. These squares
were used as temporary holders when rearranging the colors and they were empty in the
beginning of each task. On the lower right side of the screen, there was a timer that was
updated every second.
   The user’s task was to sort the colors in the big gray squares so that each big square had
the same color as the indicator square beside it. The colors were initially in mixed
positions. This initial state was the same for all subjects. The colors could be moved by
dragging and dropping them with a mouse, and they could only be dragged to empty
squares. The colors in the big gray squares were not visible unless the subject clicked on
the square, after which the color was visible for 0.4 s. A color was also visible, as the
subject started dragging it towards a different square. The subject could only swap colors
in any two big squares via the auxiliary squares at the screen center. Thus, the subject had
to perform at least four dragging movements to swap two colors and at least 32 dragging
movements to complete the task. The 12 tasks consisted of four different square layouts
and three different color orders for each layout.
2.5. Stimuli

   There were two different affective intervention categories and a condition with no
intervention The interventions were created and delivered using a Finnish speech

synthesizer program Mikropuheq version 4.2. The following parameter values were used
in creating a male voice: speed 4, pitch 2 2, peacefulness 0, random variation 0, and
interval between words 0. The goal was to create a voice that is as neutral as possible
regarding speech prosody.
   Each stimulus consisted of two sentences. The first sentence stated that the system was
not functioning properly. The emotional words were in the second sentence. In the positive
intervention condition, emotionally positive words were selected so that they would map
into the positive end of the valence dimension. In the negative intervention condition,
emotionally negative words were selected so that they would map into the negative end of
the valence dimension.
   Eight differently worded interventions were used in both intervention categories. The
placements of emotional words were varied so that the affective parts of the sentences
would not appear too similar to the subject, and the subject would have to process each
message individually. They were varied so that in both categories, there were three
interventions, in which the emotional word was at or near the beginning of the second
sentence, and three interventions, in which the emotional word was at or near the end of
the second sentence. There were also two interventions, in which there were two emotional
words: one in the beginning of the second sentence and one at or near the end of the second
sentence. All the interventions were about 4.5 s long. The positive interventions included,
for example (translated from Finnish), ‘The functions of the computer were suspended.
Great that the computer will soon work again.’ and the negative interventions included, for
example, ‘The execution of the program was interrupted. This is frustrating.’

2.6. Procedure

   As the subject entered the laboratory, she/he was seated in a comfortable chair The
subject was told that the experiment consisted of two phases: the task phase and the rating
phase. Next, the electrodes were attached, and the subject was told a cover story that while
interacting with the computer, the skin temperature of her/his face will be measured with
surface electrodes.
   First, the subject had to enter demographic information (name, age, gender) to the
computer. After that the experimental tasks were introduced to the subject. Before the
actual tasks, the subject tried one practice task until she/he reported that she/he understood
the experimental task. Thereafter, the subject was told that besides the actual tasks, she/he
would also face problematic situations during the experiment. The subject was also told
that she/he will be given comments by the computer during the experiment, and that she/he
should try to listen to the comments carefully, and try to understand the message of the
comments. To familiarize the subject to the forthcoming stimuli, the subject was first
shown a picture of an expressionless character face after which a sample of neutral speech
synthesis was played. After that, the experiment was started. This phase lasted for about
25 –30 min.
   During each task, the subject was exposed to two series of deliberately caused mouse
delays. The first delays were started randomly at 20– 30 s from the onset of the task, and
the second delays were started randomly at 15– 25 s after the end of the first series of
delays and the possible affective intervention. Each series of mouse delays lasted for 12 s

                T. Partala, V. Surakka / Interacting with Computers 16 (2004) 295–309     301

and consisted of five mouse delays of 2.32 s, and a period of 0.1 s between each pair of
delays, during which the mouse worked normally. In practice, it was impossible for the
subject to continue the task during these periods. However, the mouse cursor moved
occasionally, as the subjects kept trying to move the cursor during these periods. After
each 12-s delay period, there was a period of about 6.5 s during which first an
expressionless face was shown to signal the subject that speech communication was
soon to be expected from the computer. After one second, the speech synthesizer gave
either a positive or a negative speech intervention, and one second after the end of the
speech the face disappeared from the screen. In the no intervention condition, the
subject’s mouse was disabled during the corresponding 6.5-s period, but she/he did not
receive any intervention. After this period, in all conditions there was a three second
period, during which the mouse was disabled. After that period the mouse started
working normally again. After each task, there was a rest period of 10 s before moving
on to the next task.
   The experiment was counterbalanced so that the subjects were divided into three groups
(three females and three males in each group). The counterbalancing matrix (3
groups £ 24 intervention positions) consisted of eight consecutive 3 £ 3 Latin squares
so that two similar Latin squares were not placed consequently. This procedure ensured
the full counterbalancing of conditions and interventions during the experiment.
   After the subjects had completed all the tasks, they were instructed to rate their
subjective valence and arousal during the delay period, and in response to the no
intervention condition and the positive and negative interventions. The ratings were given
on the computer using the semantic differential method (Bradley and Lang, 1994). Two
nine-point rating scales were used for the ratings of valence and arousal. First, the subject
was instructed to rate the mouse delay period and she/he had an opportunity to try the
delay period once more. Second, the subject was instructed to rate the no intervention
condition used in the experiment on valence and arousal. Third, the subject was instructed
to listen to all the 16 intervention stimuli again, and rate her/his affective state on both
valence and arousal dimensions.

2.7. Data analysis

   The EMG data was analyzed as follows First, the EMG data was rectified. Second, eye
blinks were removed from the data. Data from all channels were discarded for all data
points if the electrical activity in the eye blink channel exceeded 40 mV. Third, the data was
baseline corrected using a three-second baseline measured before the onset of each delay
period. Fourth, the average values for the baseline corrected data were calculated for two
periods of interest: during the 6.5-s intervention period and during the three second
time period following the offset of the intervention stimuli. The average EMG activity of
the preceding delay period was subtracted from the average EMG activities of the three
conditions (i.e. negative, positive, and no intervention) for both periods of interest. This
was done, because the main interest was in the change of the EMG activity during these
periods as compared to the activity during the delay period. The behavioral data was
analyzed in terms of the number of successful dragging movements during the 10-s period
after each intervention, starting from the first mouse click on a big gray square.

4. Discussion

   The results showed that as compared to the no intervention condition, the use of
positively worded affective interventions resulted in significantly higher level
of zygomaticus major activity (i.e. smiling activity) during the interventions. During the
positive interventions, the level of smiling activity was also significantly higher than
during the negative interventions, and the smiling activity was also significantly higher
during the negative interventions than during the no intervention condition. For the data
during the 3-s period after the intervention offset, the level of smiling activity was also
significantly higher after the positive interventions than after the no intervention condition.
   For the data during the interventions, there were no statistically significant differences
between conditions in corrugator supercilii activity (i.e. frowning activity) although the
activity of this muscle site was clearly decreased during both emotional interventions.
However, the use of positively worded affective interventions resulted in significantly
decreased level of frowning activity after the interventions, as compared to the no
intervention condition.
   The analysis of the subjects’ task performance showed that positively worded
interventions resulted in significantly better task performance when compared to the
condition with no intervention. The ratings of valence and arousal showed that the subjects
rated their affective valence as more positive after the positive interventions than after the
negative interventions and after the no intervention condition.
   In general, our results are in line with the previous research. The current results for the
zygomaticus major muscle site EMG activity showed an increase in response to positively
worded interventions and a smaller increase in response to negatively worded
intervention. A similar trend has been reported earlier for emotionally positive and
negative visual material (Lang et al., 1993). Bradley and Lang (2000) studied the facial
EMG responses to affective sounds. Their results showed a significant linear dimensional
correlation between smiling activity and ratings of pleasure, but the results showed no
significant differences in smiling activity between different stimulus categories (negative,

neutral, and positive). In the current experiment, the emotional stimulus categories seemed
to elicit more pronounced smiling activity than in Bradley and Lang (2000). In the current
results, most of the differences in smiling activity between the different intervention
categories were also statistically significant.
    Previous research has suggested an inversely linear relationship between frowning
activity and ratings of valence (Bradley and Lang, 2000). In addition, they found
statistically significant differences in frowning activity as a function of stimulus category
(negative, neutral, and positive). In the current results, the only statistically significant
difference was found after the intervention between the positive intervention condition and
the no intervention condition. However, the frowning activity decreased after both positive
and negative interventions so that the decrease was somewhat larger for the positive
interventions. A similar kind of result was presented in Hietanen et al. (1998) who found
that vocal expressions with content speech prosody evoked decreased frowning activity in
the group that was classified as sensitive to emotional contagion. Decreased frowning
activity may in fact be associated with enhanced subjective valence. Thus, these results
suggest that both positively and negatively worded affective interventions enhanced the
subject’s emotions on the valence scale. Especially, the positive interventions were
helpful. In addition, the current result, which showed that the relaxation of the muscle
associated with frowning was greatest for the positive intervention category can be seen as
compatible with the results of Aula and Surakka (2002). Their results showed that the
subjects’ pupillary responses attenuated faster towards the baseline after positive task-
related feedback than after neutral and negative task-related feedback.
    The behavioral results are also in line with the previous research. For example,
according to Isen (2000) a growing body of research indicates that even mild positive
affective states can markedly influence everyday thought processes leading to improved
decision-making and problem-solving. This conclusion was supported by the current
results in which positive interventions resulted in improved problem-solving performance
in terms of the number of successful moves. Our results are also in line with the results of
Aula and Surakka (2002), who found that emotionally positive feedback in HCI provoked
shorter response times to simple mathematical problems as compared to negative
feedback. The current study also showed that the behavioral effect could also be achieved
using affective interventions related to the system malfunctions rather than feedback
related to the user’s task performance (cf. Aula and Surakka, 2002).
    The valence ratings showed that the interventions were successful in evoking emotional
experiences. In contrast to valence ratings, there were no significant differences in the
ratings of arousal for the different conditions. These results show that the current
experiment was successful in manipulating the subjects’ affective valence without
influencing the subjects’ affective arousal. Probably the use of more arousing affective
interventions would result in stronger effects. In future studies, it may be also possible to
create affective interventions specifically designed to manipulate the subjects’ arousal or
to manipulate both valence and arousal dimensions together systematically.
    The results of the current study showed that synthetic speech with emotional content
could be efficiently used in affective interventions to achieve positive effects. This is in
line with previous research. Aula and Surakka (2002) found that synthetic speech feedback
could be used to evoke different autonomic nervous system responses in the user. Similar

results have also been obtained using non-speech affective sounds (Partala et al., 2000;
Partala and Surakka, 2003). All these studies also found differences in subjective ratings of
different affective stimuli. Thus, it seems that using affective speech or affective sounds,
the subject’s emotions can be regulated in terms of both physiological activity and
subjective experiences. In addition, it seems possible to accomplish improvements in the
user’s task performance using affective speech.
   The current results have some implications for application design. The majority of the
current systems do not use any affective interventions. In the current experiment, the no
intervention condition was chosen so that the subject did not get any messages about the
system problems, which represents the state of the art in most systems, especially in the
cases the problems are not very fatal like an operating system crash. Thus, the results
suggest that it would be beneficial to incorporate affective support strategies to current
systems. For example, it would be possible for the system to notice system difficulties that
are not under user control (e.g. a failure or delays in software execution), and respond to
the difficulties in an affectively supportive way. Synthetic speech seems to be a promising
output media, but other channels such as text could be used, too. In addition, affective
interventions could be also brought to situations, in which the user has to wait for
something to be processed (e.g. long application loading times or long download times for
the Internet).
   The findings also encourage the measurement of facial expressions in human –
computer interaction. The subjects’ emotional reactions to affective interventions were
verified in the activity of two facial muscles. This suggests that the measurement of facial
expression could be useful in evaluating human – computer interaction on an affective
level. In addition, because it seems possible to measure the user’s facial expression during
human – computer interaction with enough accuracy, the measurement of facial
expressions could be used as an affective computer input signal. Thus, our results support
the view of, for example, Lisetti and Schiano (2000), who consider the measurement of
facial expressions as useful computer input.
   In this paper, we have introduced the concept of affective interventions. By doing so we
wanted to emphasize that in the current experiment the affective messages were not related
to the user’s actions or task performance. Instead, in this case the computer took the
initiative to influence on the user’s affective state. In human – computer interaction, the
transition from affective feedback to affective interventions seems to open new
possibilities. Affective interventions could be used, for example, as system-initiated
responses to changes that impede the user’s work. Affective interventions could also be
proactive, for example, they may be given before the user has encountered a certain
problem in her/his work. In addition to using affective interventions to alleviate the
effects of system problems, there are application fields, which could benefit from affective
interventions. Especially in fields in which motivation is a crucial factor, such as
computer-assisted learning, affective interventions could be assumed to have beneficial
effects.
   Clearly, a great deal of further research is needed to understand the effects of affective
interventions in different situations. In the current study, the emotional content was in the
wording of the affective messages, while the speech prosody was kept as neutral as
possible. This was enough to evoke significant changes in the subject’s physiology,

the prosody of the speech, it would be possible to achieve even stronger effects. Further,
animated agents with different gestures and facial expressions to convey additional
affective information could be used for enhancing the effects of affective interventions.
The contexts in which the interventions are given also range from feedback related to
different user tasks to interventions not directly related to the user’s task like in this

    In addition, affective interventions could be used as computer responses to significant
changes in the user’s physiology. In the field of affective computing, research work is
being done on the measurement of different physiological signals, and the recognition of
meaningful affective phenomena from the signals (e.g. Laakso et al., 2001; Partala and
Surakka, 2003; Picard, 1997; Scheirer et al., 2002). Following the knowledge on how to
recognize emotional states from affective signals, the question of how to react to the
changes in the user’s affective state becomes important. Physiological signals can be
monitored in real time by the computer, and the computer can also monitor the
physiological changes caused by its affective interventions, and adjust its behavior
accordingly. Consequently, this kind of advanced adaptive technology could even show
emotional intelligence similar to that of humans. For example, the computer could rule out
the use of interventions that cause negative user responses in the context of a particular
situation or a particular user.
    As a whole, the results of the current study showed that as compared to no intervention
condition, different physiological, behavioral, and experiential user reactions could be
evoked by using affective interventions. The results showed that significant changes in
physiology and subjective experiences could be evoked by altering the emotional wording
of the intervention. The current physiological and behavioral results can be considered as
promising. It seems that affective interventions can have positive effects to physiological
activity in general, if the user is in a negative emotional state before the intervention.
Positive-worded interventions proved to be especially helpful. Thus, based on the current
experiment, we can seriously encourage further studies on the effects of affective
interventions in human –computer interaction.
