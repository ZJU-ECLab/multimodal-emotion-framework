Abstract
Affective computing, as a novel paradigm, adds a new feature into the human-computer
interactions era that brings emotions into the process of design and evaluation. Such condition
influences digital games’ not only structural dimension but also content and evaluation areas.
This study aims to evaluate the effect of using emotions in the form of facial expressions as a
User Interface (UI) and the input of a system in virtual scenarios. After studying the findings
and methods of previous researches, a conceptual framework for Affective User Interface
Design and Evaluation (AFFUIDE) has been proposed. Then, a game in the genre of shoot
‘em up (called Facial Battle) in four interaction modes has been developed. The evaluation of
this study has been performed in terms of the players’ performance and experience. For this
purpose, 18 male students of the Multimedia Faculty of Tabriz Islamic Art University took part
in the experiments, who were asked to play all four modes of the game. Then, after analyzing
the normalization of collected data, one-way ANOVA and U Mann Whitney tests were used
for the statistical analysis. The findings indicate that the Traditional UI is the most usable and
the Full Affective UI has the best sense of fun and user experience among other methods.
However, the hybrid mode (affective shooting), is not only usable as traditional mode, but it
also works better in terms of user experience compared to other modes.

Keywords Affective Computing . Affective User Interface . Virtual Scenario . User Experience .
Facial Expressions

1 Introduction

The debate surrounding human interactions is inherently and predominantly coupled with
emotions. Humans communicate with each other not only through speech but also by using
facial expressions, body postures and expressing emotions [60]. One of the challenges of
human-computer interaction (HCI) is to bridge the gaps between the various fields of design
and implementation of human-centered computing systems [36, 76]; which is a redefinition of
the classical theory of HCI, that declares recognizing people (with their interests and activities)
should be the first priority in technology design [3]. Hence, in order to design a more natural
user interface, it is important to consider emotions in the process of human interactions with
computers and virtual scenarios [8, 28, 80].
    Previously, the primary modes of HCI only took place using traditional user interfaces such
as keyboards, mouse, sound, GUIs, and the like [56]. Although these traditional tools provide
high accuracy in the process of interactions, they are very different from the natural origins of
interpersonal interactions. With the advent of intelligent technologies in the field of image and
sound processing and recent advancement of various types of sensors, it has been possible to
create more natural and emotional interactions between humans and computers [25]. Electro-
encephalography (EEG), Electrocardiography (ECG), Galvanic Skin Response (GSR), Respi-
ration Rate Analysis (RR), Skin Temperature Measurements (SKT), Electromyogram (EMG),
Electrooculography (EOG), Facial Expressions (FE), Body Posture (BP), and Gesture Anal-
ysis (GA) are the main emotion recognition approaches that have been evolved in sensor
technology and methods in recent years for affective HCI [22]. Hybrid approaches [77],
multimodal features and even the text that is typed in by users during a game or social media
communication can serve as a possible starting point for the affect detection process and
sentiment analysis in digital environments [13]. Purpose, cost, intrusion, accuracy, user
tolerance, and the computing processing power are the important elements involved in
selecting and utilizing the sensing tools in each project [29]. Among these approaches, facial
expression analysis as a non-invasive and contactless method is applicable for mass and
individual emotion recognition [22] and game industry [58], which can be used easily through
a simple webcam and a trained algorithm [2].
    However, in addition to the significant advances in machine learning and data mining
continuation in improving the accuracy and efficiency of data analysis and emotion recogni-
tion algorithms [73, 26, 53, 65], more attention should be paid to crucial elements of user
experience (UX) in interaction design [4]. Designing natural and especially affective interfaces
in the field of computer games, in addition to having a positive impact on emotional and
psychological communication, improves user-product interaction [54]. This study aims to
investigate the effect of using emotions in the form of facial expressions as a user interface
and input of a computer game system.
    In spite of various studies mentioned above, there has not been a deep investigation in the
usability and user experience of utilizing emotions in the form of facial expressions - like
anger, fear, surprise, disgust, joy and sadness - as a User Interface and input of a computer
game system. This paper aims to answer these questions whether using emotions in the form of
facial expressions can be used as a user interface and input of a computer game system, and be
replaced with each other or not. Also, it tries to find out what effect can such a natural UI have
on players’ performance and experience. For this regard, after performing a systematic
literature review, a conceptual framework for Affective User Interface Design and Evaluation
(AFFUIDE) is proposed in this study. Then, based on this framework, a shoot ‘em up game,
Multimedia Tools and Applications (2021) 80:5225–5253                                      5227


called Facial Battle, in four modes of user interface is developed. The evaluation of this study
has been carried out in two quantitative and subjective ways that include users’ performance,
which is logged during play, and their experience, which is obtained through questionnaires.
For the second part, three questionnaires of GAMEFULLQUEST [31], SUS [9], and
AttrakDiff [30] was used to assess immersion, system usability scale, and detailed user
experience and user acceptance, respectively. For this purpose, 18 male students of the
Multimedia Faculty of Tabriz Islamic Art University took part in the experiments, who were
asked to play all four modes of the game and fill in the related questionnaires. Then, after
analyzing the normalization of collected data through Smirnov Kolmogorov test, one-way
ANOVA and Mann Whitney U tests were used for the statistical analysis. The overall
contributions of this research are:
& Proposing a conceptual framework for affective user interface design and evaluation
    (AFFUIDE).
& Design and development of an innovative form of interaction in a casual game using the
    facial expression as an entry mode.
& Conducting a user study to evaluate the proposed framework in terms of players’ perfor-
    mance and experience.


2 Related work

Picard describes Affective Computing as the ability of a computing device to recognize,
interpret, and simulate human affective states [60]. This affective state is an emotion which
is an inseparable part of human intelligence [28]. Despite a large number of various definitions
of emotion, many agree that emotion is a reaction to events that are deemed relevant to the
needs and goals of an individual [39]. Also, many psychological models of affect have been
provided. The most commonly agreed-upon list of basic emotions consists of anger, disgust,
fear, joy, sadness and surprise, which is also known as Ekman’s discrete model of emotions
[23]. Also, emotion can be represented by two values for valence and arousal, like Lang’s
dimensional model of emotion [43]; or, it can be presented by more than two values like
Plutchick’s multidimensional [61] or Russell’s circumplex model [68]. All of these models can
be utilized for different fields of affective computer interactions.
    Although a variety of studies and extensive surveys have been done in the field of affective
computing in digital games, this study aims to have not only an overall review of them but also
a detailed investigation in the UI area of this domain.

2.1 Affective computing in digital games

The most prominent goal of affective computing in digital games is to create systems with
natural user interfaces in order to bridge the gap between technology and human activities
by processing and responding to users’ affective needs [16]. In order to build a relationship
between the user’s affective states and structures of the games, different types of interac-
tions come into consideration. An individual’s emotional state and his body’s physiological
responses are often connected to each other. Some of these responses from the skin, the
brain, or the heart are more automatic than be controllable by a user; however, on the other
hand, facial expressions or body movements can be controlled on one’s own free will.
Recognizing a person’s affect-related data for affective interaction can be through sensing
5228                                                      Multimedia Tools and Applications (2021) 80:5225–5253


physiological data, observing the body, analyzing voice, or analyzing text as illustrated in
Fig. 1. [28]. The methods of this purpose are in two groups of invasive and non-invasive
ways and sensors. Although invasive methods can provide more accurate and reliable data
during an interaction, non-invasive ones are less disrupting [42]. Many elements such as
purpose, cost, intrusion, accuracy, user tolerance, and the computing processing power are
involved in selecting and utilizing the sensing tools in each project [29]. After selecting the
technical type of interaction, methodologies from affective computing can be used by
digital game designers and developers for user experience evaluation [20, 48], game
adaption [5], game advance tool [44], research [67], player’s emotion manipulation [18],
and attention modification [51] through the structure and elements of a game, which are
divided into three sections: “dynamics”, “mechanics,” and “components” [82]. Given this
issue and research in this field, the presence of affective computing in digital games can be
categorized into four areas of narrative, interface, the player’s avatar, and non-player
characters (NPCs) [16].
    Within this field, affect is used in the creation of believable characters [7, 12], dynamic
difficulty adjustment of game level [46, 27], flow improvement [38], enjoyment enhance-
ment [85], affective behavior design for virtual characters [83, 14], learning motivation
increase [66, 69], and competition provoke [32]. The use of affect in digital games has led
to the development of more convincing and richer narratives [35]. Such utilization as a
decision input tool in determining the type of narrative can positively affect players’
emotional engagement, sense of empathy, and consequently their enjoyment [79] and
increase the playability of games [78]. Also, affect-based interaction between players and
NPCs results in the more immersion of him/her in the game [34]. In the following, the focus
of research is to investigate main relative previous works and highlight their contribution
based on the classification proposed in Fig. 1.
    In [57], Partala evaluated the use of affective information in HCI. One of the goals of his
research was to use facial affective states to estimate the amount of user arousal and valence,
and then, use it as a source of affective information to simulate a virtual human-like conver-
sational character in computer output. In this regard, he investigated this issue by measuring
the activity of the facial muscles of zygomaticus major (the muscle that draws the lip corners

up producing a smile) and corrugator supercilii (the muscle that knits and lowers the brows
producing a frown), which are strongly related with an emotional experience. The result of his
study indicates that facial expressions, as a standard way of an individual’s affective arousal
and valence amount estimation, can be used to establish emotional relationships with the
computer. Through Partala’s study, it is inferable that by recognizing players’ affective state
and manipulating NPCs’ emotional feedbacks based on these recognized emotions, their felt
affective interaction and proximity with the content of a virtual scenario or narrative could be
highly influenced - the more simulated level of emotions of NPCs based on the players’
emotion, the more affective interaction results.
    In [44], Lankes et al. proposed a game by the name of “EmoFlowers” to investigate the
utilization of facial expressions as game input with different emotional feedback conditions.
Their study conducted in a shopping center, where the players must stand in front of a monitor
and adopt a specific facial expression to advance the game. In the scenario of the game, by
means of specific emotions, players could change the weather state and influence the growth of
a virtual flower. Two basic emotions of joy and sadness employed in their gameplay, which
were used to cause sunshine and rain respectively. Their finding of players’ UX assessment
declares that interaction with a game via facial expression not only provides a positive
experience but also impact on their emotional disposition effectively. This study provides
good views on the methodology and game design of affective games and indicates that using
facial expressions as a user interface and game input with different emotional feedback
conditions via adaption in scenario and narrative can improve the emotional state of players.
    In [40], Korn et al. investigated numerous ways of procedural algorithms incorporation in
computer games to evaluate the effect of procedural content generation on UX versus
manually designed one. They found that procedural content generation can create an experi-
ence that users perceive as both significantly more realistic and significantly more aesthetically
pleasing. Such procedural content generation can be utilized for affective based procedural
content generation in games, too; such as [71] for attention training, or like [33] for
emotionally-adaptive virtual reality mental health applications.
    In [72], Sekhavat et al. conducted a study on emotion awareness for player experience by
investigating the role of the affect of others to one’s gameplay performance in a car racing
multiplayer game. They evaluated the impact of the expression of manifested affects of an
opponent on the emotions, and consequently the gameplay behavior of players. They showed
that the emotional state of the opponent expressed by an emoji can not only attract the attention
of players, but also affect the game behavior of players in terms of speeding up, reducing
speed, collisions, and gameplay duration. Furthermore, they showed that the expression of
manifested emotions of an opponent can affect the emotions of players in terms of increasing
the rate of their emotional state changes. They concluded that affective interaction not only can
influence player’s emotion and lead to an increase in the perceived competitiveness and
enjoyment of the game, but also can be utilized as an experience evaluation tool during play.
    In [55], Obaid et al. developed a game by the name of “Feed the Fish” to investigate the
dynamic difficulty adjustment based on the emotional feedback of a player in the form of facial
expressions. In this game, the player in the role of a fish must catch and eat smaller fish and
escape the bigger predatory ones. The sadder the player is, the easier the game will be and
reciprocally, the happier the player is, harder and more challengeable the game will be. Among
22 subjects, the quantitative results of the analysis of variance of players’ performance did not
show a significant difference between the two game modes. On the other hand, the results of
subjective analysis of the players’ experience through 4 questions on the level of ease,
5230                                             Multimedia Tools and Applications (2021) 80:5225–5253


enjoyment, attractiveness and performance of the players indicate that there is a significant
difference in the enjoyment and attractiveness and no significant difference in its performance
and ease. So, based on their findings, using emotions for adaption via dynamic difficulty
adjustment of games influence player’s emotion and improves the fun level of the game rather
than players’ performance. Generalizing this study into the affective user interface era suggests
that the harder the game, the less usability can result in UI.
    In [34], Ilves et al. evaluated the possibility of using a combination of head movement and
emotions as a game input. The proposed game aimed to guide a drunkard to his home without
falling down or hitting available obstacles on his way. By moving the head to left or right, the
game character tilts to the same side. Emotions were also utilized to interact with NPCs in the
game. It used to smile to make moving NPCs stand in their place, and frown to make
mesmerizing ones scare and run away. Also, another version of the game used traditional
keyboard and gamepad input for this interaction. The result of their study indicates positive
feedback in the entertainment dimension of the game with the first mode. They have
acknowledged that using emotions to interact with NPCs leads to more natural interaction
and also increases immersion in the game, but the traditional user interface (keyboard and
gamepad) is better reported than using emotions as input by users. But, as one of their main
limitations of study, which calls into question the results of their research, was their weak
emotion recognition system. We think the ability of an emotion recognition system can affect
the felt usability of such UI.
    In [1], Andersson explored the possibility of using head movement and facial features (lip
corners and eyebrows) to advance a platform-style game. In this game, by tilting the lip corner
to the left or right, the character moves to the same side subsequently and jumps by raising
eyebrows. By evaluating the number of jumps, turns, and the average time the user needs to
complete a level between four modes of using the facial feature, head movement, a combina-
tion of them and just traditional keyboard mode. They reported better performance with the
traditional interface than the other ones. However, there was no statistically significant
difference in the combination mode of facial feature and head movement input with traditional
one, the level of entertainment and enjoyment of using the facial feature and head movement
was higher. Finally, they found it inappropriate to replace the full use of proposed facial
features and head movements with traditional interfaces. In this study, In spite of detailed
exploration among the four mentioned modes, there has not been any study in the hybrid use
of facial features with traditional input devices.
    In [52], Muller et al. implemented a Jump’n’Run game using the facial expression as
control input for entertaining children during car journeys. Emotional facial expressions
of joy, surprise and disgust were used for three mechanisms of jumping, bending and
running in their game by the name of “Arch’n’Smile.” The game was played on a
monitor behind the front seats for children, and they conducted their study with children
and their parents in some real driving situations. This study not only lacks statistical
analysis and evaluation of the user experience of the designed game but also the
designed game does not provide any meaningful feedback to the players. But in the
absence of such evaluations, oral interviews of the researchers with children and their
parents indicated that such game’ inputs are more fun and less dizzy-making during car
journeys than previous ones.

   2.2 Affective user interface

In order to bridge the gaps between the various fields of human-centered computing systems
design, it is important to consider emotions in the process of human interactions with
computers and virtual scenarios, and provide a more natural user interface, or in other words
an Affective User Interface. There has not yet been provided an independent definition for the
Affective User Interface; however, it can be defined as Picard’s definition of affective
computing [59] and the overall definition of a user interface [62]:


   The occurrence space of interactions between humans and machines, which is ‘related
   to,‘ ‘arises from’ or ‘influences’ emotions.
To create an Affective User Interface, a cycle among different aspects of digital games should
be created. Game players’ affective states play a major role in immersive virtual environments.
Capturing and reflecting the player’s emotional state in a game, through adaptive elements
such as music, images, and stories can improve the aesthetic dimensions of it [10]. For this
purpose, the designing manner of the elements of a game UI, which plays an effective role in
motivating the users and engaging them with the game, has a direct relationship with the
degree of enjoyment of the game [47, 81], which should be considered for better both aesthetic
and UX dimensions. In affective user interface, the data collection for affective state recogni-
tion play a vital role to create an integrated cycle of affective interaction, which should be
selected for this field in a suitable manner and preferably a non-invasive method. On the other
hand, if a true behavior analysis be used during this cycle, Affective User Interface can be
utilized in every states of a virtual scenario system based on the user proficiency and dynamic
input adjustment. Furtherly, providing meaningful affective responses to the players can result
in better affective interaction. However, previous studies in spite of providing valuable
research in this field, have not presented any practical framework to cover these aspects of
Affective User Interface development what we have proposed in the next section.


3 Proposed framework

After investigating previous researches and their methods, this study presents a conceptual
framework (Fig. 2) for Affective User Interface Design and Evaluation (AFFUIDE). Basically,
this conceptual framework consists of six main sections including User Condition, Affective
State Recognition as Input, Virtual Scenario System, Data Collection, Input Condition, and
Output. The detailed definition of each one is described in the following.

User condition In a system associated with Affective User Interface, the user’s emotional and
affective conditions (S1) are a part of the interaction cycle. After the cycle starts, these
conditions are used to Analyze User Proficiency & Behavior [37], Dynamic Input Adjustment
[21], and Input Mode Determination [63] via another system (S2) which is, in general, an
algorithm to produce a minimum data for game advance. S2 can be decided by the developer
to be adjustable by the user before the game starts, or to evoke during the play automatically.
In this framework, the classification of user’s condition is on the base of Ekman’s discrete
model of emotions, which not only logs, but also play a control role during the game.
5234                                                  Multimedia Tools and Applications (2021) 80:5225–5253




Fig. 2 The conceptual framework of Affective User Interface Design and Evaluation (AFFUIDE)


Data collection for affective state recognition as input This section shows the detection
and interpretation of the user’s emotions in order to be used in the system. User’s
emotion detection is possible through many ways. Sensing physiological data, observing
the body, analyzing voice and text are the primary methods used to convey the affective
data of users in virtual scenarios such as serious or entertainment games [28, 22].
Purpose, cost, intrusion, accuracy, user tolerance, and the computing processing power
are the important elements involved in selecting and utilizing the sensing tools in each
project [29]. This study uses Ekman’s discrete classification of emotions, which clas-
sifies human emotions into the 6 basic emotions of joy, sadness, fear, disgust, anger and
surprise [23]. Supposing particular facial expressions to these emotions among human
beings strengthens the desire to use this model instead of other ones such as Lang
dimensional [43] or Plutchik multidimensional [61] emotion classifications [79]. For this
reason, the proposed system uses a simple webcam for emotion detection via facial
expressions of the user, which is considered as an affordable and non-invasive method
[11]. Affectiva SDK has been used for this purpose in this project. By analyzing facial
action units, this SDK can correctly identify 9 emotional states and 15 facial expressions
with an average accuracy of over 90% and does not need powerful processing resources
[50]. Figure 3 shows an overview of how this SDK works.

Virtual scenario system Any interactive simulated virtual environment such as VR, AR,
digital game and the like do have a simple or complex virtual scenario. The sequence of
interconnected scenarios creates a system of states in the virtual environment that we call it a
virtual scenario system. A game system, in this case, consists of Dynamics, Mechanics, and
Components [82] that the combination of these three elements forms the states of this virtual
scenario. Emotions can be used as various user interfaces in these states. In an abstract manner,
these occurrence space of interactions can be modeled as inputs from the user and outputs from
the system. The overall types of these inputs can be classified into State-based Input (SI) and
Impact based Input (II) which have been proposed by [63].

Data log This section logs the required data in the time series for qualitative and quantitative
analysis of the system. The collected data indicates the user proficiency and behavior, which
are also used for dynamic input adjustment [85, 70] and as input information for S2. Logging
the user’s facial behavior and analyzing intentional or unintentional emotional variations
during a virtual scenario provides more possibilities for UX evaluations and following
affective interaction improvement.

Input condition A virtual scenario’s input is a piece of information such as data, commands,
etc., which is passed to the system at run time from an external source, which is used in some
way to modify internal data [63, 74]. The availability of choice and personalization elements
can have a positive effect on user experience [37]. The determination of input type, in terms of
scenario, can be Required (IC1), Optional (IC2), or Selection (IC3) [63]. This section
determines the use of the affective user interface directly (Active), indirectly (Passive), or in
a combination of these two modes. In Active mode, the user must try to adopt or control the
emotion in order to advance the game. In passive mode, some of the game’s elements are
changed by the player’s real-time emotion, implicitly. The Input Condition (IC) is affected by
the Input Mode (IM) phase which is determined based on the game level, the player’s skill and
his behavioral performance.

Output All information and responses provided by the system in a variety of ways such as
audio, video, and the like are included in the output [63]. In affective user interface, it is
predictable that a responsive and reciprocal emotional interaction in front of the users’
expressed emotions, results in better user experience than unilateral and mechanical feedback.
Such emotional feedback can be provided through sound [19], environment [46], NPCs [45],
or player’s graphical avatar as used in Facial Battle.

3.1 Game design

Based on the AFFUIDE, an affective user interface can be designed and established in a
variety of ways in the occurrence space of interactions between human and the system of
digital games. Investigating all possible scenarios requires multiple studies and is beyond
the scope of one research. However, the present study, in order to evaluate and test its
hypotheses, has developed a game in the genre of Shoot’ em up by the name of “Facial
Battle”. Since the presented UI is innovative in its kind, such a genre of game is utilized due
to its explicit mechanism to prevent the influence of interference variables on the user
experience like unclear goals, rules or skill. In this game, an aircraft on the left side of the
screen can be moving up or down and fire at the monsters entering as the “enemy” from the
5236                                                  Multimedia Tools and Applications (2021) 80:5225–5253


right side. The user, in the role of the pilot of the aircraft, must shoot at the enemy. Each
mode of the game is 2 minutes and 5 “Lives” are intended for the player during this time,
which ends if he/she be hit 5 times by the enemy. Also, 5 “Spells” are intended to aid the
player in necessary times he/she wants, in which each time eight bombs fall vertically on
enemies.
   In order to compare the affective UI with the traditional one, Facial Battle is designed in 4
modes. In these modes, the use of “Affective UI” and “Traditional UI” influences on the
mechanisms of the aircraft, which are described in the following.

Traditional user interface mode (Full traditional) Aircraft’s moving up and down is done
with and buttons on the keyboard. Also, the shooting action is done by the left mouse button
and the spells are activated by the shift key on the keyboard.

Affective user interface mode (Full affective) Aircraft’s moving up and down is done by
producing of “Surprise” and “Anger” facial expressions. Also, the shooting action is done by
producing “Joy” and the spells are activated by producing “Fear” facial expressions.

Hybrid user interface mode I (Affective shooting) Aircraft’s moving up and down is done
with and buttons on the keyboard. The shooting action is done by producing “Joy” and the
spells are activated by producing “Fear” facial expressions.

Hybrid user interface mode II (Affective movement) Aircraft’s moving up and down is done
by producing of “Surprise” and “Anger” facial expressions. The shooting action is done by the
left mouse button and the spells are activated by the shift key on the keyboard. Selecting
corresponding facial expressions for mentioned actions were based on relevant facial action
coding system (FACS) [24], however, are interchangeable. Also, the player could see a guide
for input mechanism in the game by hovering his mouse cursor on each mode before starting
the game. For example, the guidance image of Affective Shooting is illustrated in Fig. 4.

4 Study

This research is a descriptive-experimental study with an applied approach. The subjective and
quantitative evaluations of this study have been on the base of users’ performance and their
experience investigation. Eighteen participants invited to take part in the experiments, who
were recruited from the students of the Faculty of Multimedia at Tabriz Islamic Art University.
Their ages ranged from 21 to 37 years old, with a mean of 26.75 years. Upon their attendance,
after a succinct description of the tests, we asked them to read a consent form and sign it, if
they agreed to participate in our tests. Participants completed a brief demographic question-
naire regarding their age, gender, video game skill level, left or right-handed, vision status,

5 Discussion

Intrinsic emotional communication between humans necessitates the study of affective human-
computer interaction methods and techniques in the digital games’ era. This study, beyond the
one-way perspective of previous researches, provided a framework for Affective User Inter-
face Design and Evaluation (AFFUIDE) and developed the “Facial Battle” game to evaluate
the using of emotions in the form of facial expressions as a user interface in games.

   Intrusion” indicated not only a semi- equivalent performance of “Full Traditional”,
   “Affective Shooting”, and “Affective Movement”, but also a better performance than
   “Full Affective”. Thus, it can be concluded that the mere use of emotion as input leads to
   low performance of users, but on the other hand, a combination of traditional and Full
   Affective yields not only a semi-equivalent result in performance but also better user
   experience. So, based on such results and mentioned subjective evaluations, it can be
   concluded that using facial expressions and combining them with traditional interaction
   techniques to advance virtual scenarios as input can be feasible, and given its impact on
   user experience, it can have synergistic influence and play a significant role in HCI.
2. The results of the statistical analysis of “Fired Bullets” in comparing all modes with each
   other, was not statistically significant and the average of Fired Bullets are the same in all 4
   modes. It means that the use of Joy instead of Left Mouse Button has a similar perfor-
   mance. Also, as they have a semi-equivalent pragmatic quality with each other, more
   hedonic quality of using Joy as input can play an important role in interactions with the
   game system.
3. The results of the statistical analysis of “Used Spells” in “Full Affective vs. Full Tradi-
   tional”, “Full Affective vs. Affective Movement”, “Full Traditional vs. Affective
Multimedia Tools and Applications (2021) 80:5225–5253                                       5249


   Shooting”, and “Affective Shooting vs. Affective Movement”, respectively, were statis-
   tically significant. By examining the mean of this variable, it can be seen that the players
   have used more spells via facial expression than the keyboard. It means that in spite of
   probable different strategies of players, they could establish good relations with such
   state-based input.
4. The results of the last single question and according to quantitative results, it can be found
   that using of a particular keyboard button in Full Traditional for some state-based inputs,
   like spell in this case, is a bit difficult for players, but by assigning this task to facial
   expression, a better result can be achieved.


5.3 A discussion around the limitations of the study

Although this study has tried to have a comprehensive evaluation on the UX elements, any
change in the dynamic, mechanic, or the component of the presented game can have a
significant impact on user experience. As Obaid et al. mentioned in [55], the difficulty of
the game could have a significant impact on the usability of the different modalities, which
means the harder the game, the less usability can result in UI. In such conditions, the perceived
usability vary in each scenario and makes it necessary to pay enough attention in the balance of
Traditional and Affective UI’s as game inputs. We aim to address this issue in the future work
by taking into account different difficulty levels in the game.
   One limitation of our study was using only male participants in the study. According to the
results of the pre-study questionnaire, most of the players were professional or semiprofes-
sional who play video games at least once a week. As most of gamers are male in our society,
we preferred to evaluate our hypothesis on males. However, for the results to be generalizable
to the whole society, a new insight could emerge analyzing the interaction with female
participants in our future work.


6 Conclusions and future work

This paper wanted to answer these questions whether using emotions in the form of facial
expressions can be used as a user interface and input of a computer game system, and be
replaced with each other or not. We also aimed to find what effect such a natural UI can have
on players’ performance and experience. For this regard, this paper proposed a conceptual
framework for affective user interface design and evaluation (AFFUIDE) based on the findings
of previous studies via a systematic review on the classification of affective interaction in
digital games. We developed and evaluated a game called Facial Battle in four user interface
modes. In this game, the player in the role of the pilot of the aircraft moving up or down in the
left side should fire at the monsters entering as the enemy from the right side. The four modes
of this game’s user interfaces consisted of traditional user interface which was through
keyboard and mouse, affective user interface which was through facial expressions of joy,
anger, surprise, and fear, and two hybrid modes of these two input methods. The evaluation of
this study was performed in two quantitative and subjective ways that include users’ perfor-
mance and their experience. Time resistance, score, player health, enemy intrusion, fired
bullet, and used spells in each mode were the elements which were used for the quantitative
evaluation of the designed system and user performance. On the other hand, for subjective
5250                                                   Multimedia Tools and Applications (2021) 80:5225–5253


evaluations, to evaluate the immersion of each mode of game, the immersion section of the
GameFullQuest questionnaire was used, consisting of 10 questions in which answers were
based on the 5-point Likert scale. Furthermore, the AttrakDiff questionnaire was used to
evaluate the user experience and user acceptance with the usability approach, and System
usability scale (SUS) questionnaire to obtain a global perspective of subjective assessments of
usability. The results indicate that the Traditional UI is the most usable and the Affective UI
has the best sense of fun and user experience among other methods, but their hybrid mode
(Affective Shooting), not only is as usable as traditional UIs, but it also works better in terms of
user experience compared to other modes. Also, the findings show that using emotions based
on facial expressions as the control input is an interesting concept in the field of digital games.
However, introducing the proposed AFFUIDE is at the beginning of the road and needs more
investigations on different aspects.
   In the future work, to investigate the concept in more detail, we will extend the game with
additional features and more affective interaction types. Affective UI seems to be a useful
intervention to aid the emotional development of children with Autism Spectrum Disorder
(ASDs). It is predictable that using such UI in multiplayer local games can produce a joyful
atmosphere in the environment between players, which can not only have positive impact in
entertainment, but also in the serious game domain. Also, from a technical point of view, we
will try to evaluate different states of a virtual environments, different levels of difficulties,
various types of feedbacks, affective based procedural content generation and many other
elements that may come into play in this field, in our future studies. Finally, as an innovative
interaction type, affective user interface is expected to be welcomed by people who are unable
to use their hands. This application of affective user interface can be generalized in many
multimedia tools to provide more possibilities for players with disabilities to enjoy playing
games and interact with virtual worlds.
