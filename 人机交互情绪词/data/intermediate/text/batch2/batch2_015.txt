1. Introduction

    In the study of Human–Computer Interaction (HCI) and psychology, emotion recognition has a wide range of prospective
applications. Emotion is a psycho-physiological process triggered by the conscious and unconscious perception of a situation or an
object and is often associated with mood, temperament, personality, disposition, and motivation [1]. The emotion of an individual
plays a central role in everyday decisions and well-being. Developing systems for identifying and managing human emotion becomes
an integral domain in HCI research [2]. However, identifying human emotion during HCI addresses several challenges such as natural
experimental setup, the non-contact user interface for interactive interaction, induce real feeling, and real-time recommendation
system. Automatically recognizing and responding to a user’s emotion during interactions with a computer can enhance the quality
of the communication to maximize the user’s well-being. Furthermore, the methods, models, and frameworks developed in the
field of HCI used to sense human psychological states can supply specific valuable references to human emotion. Humans react
emotionally to specific situations provoking some physiological changes that can be recognized using various modality such as
facial expressions, facial blood flow, speech, behavior (gesture/posture), and physiological signals. Emotions can trigger some small
changes in facial blood flow with an impact on skin temperature. The facial blood flow rate, variations in body temperature, and
muscle are immensely emotion-specific. In previous studies infrared thermal face localization, feature extraction methods for emotion

1.1. Research objective


   In this research work, the principal objective of this work is to develop a framework for computing the multivariate time series
thermal video sequences to recognize human emotion and provide relevant psychological suggestions. We achieved this objective
using a three-stage framework.
Stage I : Detection and tracking of face ROIs in thermal video.
Stage II: Applied the Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) algorithm to classify the dominant emotional states.
Stage III : Recommend the relevant psychological suggestions.


2. Literature review


    In an intelligent HCI system, accurately recognizing the human emotional states in real-time is the prime concern. So that the
interaction between the human and computer should be more intelligent and suggest the relevant distraction. Human emotion
can be recognized either using verbal (explicit channel) or non-verbal (implicit channel) cues [9,10]. The non-verbal approach
divided broadly into three categories according to a different modality of signal: (1) Audio–visual [11,12], (2) Physiological, and
(3) Multimodal. Physiological signal based on contact or invasive approach needs pasting or binding sensors in the human body,
which makes the human comfort limited and restricted due to the external factors such as movement. But the non-contact or non-
invasive process of emotion recognition employing audio–visual signal (facial expression, facial temperature, and speech or audio)
aims to increase the accuracy irrespective of low computational complexity, low computational cost, and medium robustness [13].
Significant progress has been made for automatic facial expression recognition in the visible domain [14,15]. Due to the misleading
of human facial expression, the visual image fails to recognize the real emotion. In real-life scenario, the facial movements do
not always match with how human express and to perceive facial emotions. So, the researchers have recently started studying the
thermal modality for real emotion recognition. The facial temperature variation devised through face accumulation branches is not
understanding to lighting conditions in infrared thermal images. Thus, identification of emotional states through thermal modality
is an essential harmonious to emotion recognition using the visible modality [16,17]. However, for this work the important aspects
of emotion recognition using thermal video sequences in a real-time HCI framework are thermal face and ROIs registration, tracking
of facial ROIs, real emotion recognition, and relevant distraction suggestions.


2.1. Thermal face registration and facial ROIs tracking


    In a dynamic image sequence-based method, there are various machine learning and deep learning architecture used for detecting
the thermal face and facial ROIs [18]. Faster R-CNN architecture is considered for thermal face and ROIs registration after certain
comparison concerning the test time per thermal image, computational speed, and other limitations of CNN, R-CNN, Fast R-CNN,
and Faster R-CNN. This approach is popular in deep learning where pre-trained models used faster_rcnn_inception_v2_coco [18]. To
identify the region proposals on the feature map, a dedicated network is used to predict the region called Region Proposal Network
(RPN), instead of using selective search algorithms. The possibility of a bounding box being background or foreground, and refine
the bounding boxes are predicted by RPN. The recent advances in face detection stimulated several successful real-time tracking
algorithms [19]. In previous studies of object detection and object locations are unknown difficult which are often not discussed.
However, by using traditional methods, Viola et al. object detection has inherent ambiguities that make it more complex to train a
classifier. We suggested using a Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) [20] approach for object tracking. The face detector trained with
weaker labeling (just the center of the face) and this approach outperforms a state of the supervised art algorithm trained with
explicit bounding boxes is stated in the pioneering study of Boris Babenko et al.

2.2. Real emotion recognition and relevant distraction suggestions

    In current studies of emotion recognition, the outer facial expression or inner affective detection using thermal images has drawn
the attention [21]. During HCI, automatic emotion recognition has very realistic and great significance for harmonious and natural
as compared with posed facial expression recognition [9]. The temporal knowledge in infrared thermal images is an essential cue for
real expression recognition, which is not considered by Khan et al. studies. Abhiram Kolli et al. proposed an emotion identification
approach using fused features of radon transform, wavelet transforms, Histogram of Oriented Gradient (HOG) features, and K-Nearest
Neighbors (KNN). In some of the previous studies suggested a method to classify emotion based on Facial Thermal Feature Points
(FTFPs) by analyzing segment-wise temperature variation using Principal Component Analysis (PCA). Also, Stephanos Ioanno et al.
addressed that, thermal imagery seems to provide new avenues for the study of emotions. Spontaneous facial patch detection can
closely match with human manual interpretation in recent deep learning approaches [22]. While these approaches are well-validated
on high-fidelity, application to thermal imagery and full-color facial images has been mostly unexplored.
    Negative emotions heightened a common response to upsetting thoughts and images, which stimulus the cognition and traps us
in distressing cycles that can be hard to break [23]. Distraction can serve two purposes such as breaking unhelpful cycles of thoughts
or images that might otherwise result in negative moods and changing attitudes toward negative cognition. Distraction has shown
to have direct therapeutic benefits, both physically and psychologically [24]. Various distraction techniques, which include physical
exercise, refocusing, mental exercise, and counting cognition used in previous studies [23]. But for this paper research scope, we used
our customized software module designed using python and integrated with our proposed framework. After the classification result
obtained from stage-II, our customized application will provide some relevant psychological suggestions like breathing exercise,
kriya yoga meditation, and attending counseling center, which described in stage-III.

3. Methods and materials

    Real emotion recognition is merely confined to the control environment and discretionary participation of subjects in the
experiment. A sample of 67 participants was engaged from the student population of IIT, Kharagpur, out of which 2 were removed
due to incomplete data. In sum, data from 65 participants were analyzed 𝜇 = 24.18, 𝜎 = 2.73, out of which 35 males and 30
females. Exclusion criteria included any ongoing physical or mental health problems and or use of medications. All the participants
signed an informed consent form before taking part in the study in a separate room prior to watch the video stimuli and filled
the General Health Questionnaire (GHQ)-28 on the same day of the experiment. The participants were informed that they had to
involve in an experiment for the assessment of the emotional content and intensity of the video clips. For the accurate assessment of
their emotion, the participants had undergone a viewing and experiencing the emotions. At the end of each video clip, participants
were informed to fill a Self Assessment Manikin (SAM), which is a categorical feedback form on self-experienced emotion, that the
video clip communicated and the intensity with which they felt them on a scale of zero (0) to five (5). The self-evaluation report
confers insight into the expressiveness of the subjects to different emotion categories which is provided in Fig. 3. As per the recorded
statistics of the emotional intensity of six emotions of 65 participants, the anger videos were the intensity with 0.56, disgust with
4.28, fear with 1.43, joy with 4.72, sad with 4.19, and surprise with 1.67 respectively. Therefore, we considered the thermal facial
recording of disgust, joy, and sad emotion for further experimental analysis. A baseline recording preceded each video recording for
a stimuli-driven case and the participants were also informed that they had the right to quit the experiment at any point in time.
They were debriefed about the purpose of the study at the end of the experiment. The Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) of IIT,
Kharagpur approved the experimental methods and materials along with the video stimulus shown to the participants.

3.1. Design of experiment and data synchronization

    As shown in Fig. 1, a made-to-order wooden box was designed along with other peripherals. A dedicated room with the controlled
temperature and illumination at 22 ◦ C and 150 lux respectively was used. For recording the visual videos, one web camera with
specifications 30 fps at 1280 × 720 is used. The recorded videos have been stored in the .mp4 format with x264 codec. With
fixed focal length, 80 × 60 resolution, and 9 fps, one infrared camera is utilized to record the thermal videos. One customized
software application (Python script) is utilized to record both the visual and thermal facial data of a participant’s concurrently.
Considering the different frame rates of cameras, we maintain a log file to match the timestamps. As per the assessment guideline
of the counseling center of IIT, Kharagpur, the emotional states present in the recorded visual videos were extracted by screening
more than 60 prospective decoders. Out of which, six decoders, three of each gender, were selected on the basis of their experience
to detect emotions, Micro Expression Training Tool and Subtle Expression Training Tool [25]. We have considered thermal video
clips of the same timestamp maintained in the case of visual video through a log file.

3.2. Selection of video stimuli

   The video clips for inducing the emotions were (5 to 8 min in duration) identified, based on our pilot studies as well as from the
video clips used in previous studies [26,27]. The selected video clips were rated on a scale of 1–5 (1: Not suitable 5: Most suitable)
by ten volunteers and accordingly contemplate in our experimental process. Videos eliciting multiple emotions were rejected, which
may lead to a mixed emotional expression. The videos were included for six discrete emotions such as Anger, Disgust, Fear, Joy,
Sad, and Surprise followed by a neutral video of one minute. The sample synchronized thermal and visual videos of participants
are shown in Fig. 2.

4.1.3. Multivariate time series sequence extraction
    The temperature of the object encoded as the intensity ranging from 0 to 255 in the thermal image. The mean intensity variation
of facial patches shown in 10, implies the mean temperature variation across the frames. The Facial ROIs tracked and their mean
intensity variation stored in a .csv format for future analysis. The mean intensity variation of all six ROIs considered as a multivariate
time series (MTS) data. A time series is a sequence of analytical data points in successive order. If only one variable is varying over
time, then it is called a uni-variate time series. In contrast, if more than one variable is changing over time, it is called a multivariate
time series.

4.1.4. Kalman filtering of MTS sequences
   The Kalman filter uses dynamic systems modeling and multiple sequential computations to form a view of the system’s varying
quantities that is better than the estimation obtained by using only one measurement alone [29]. The algorithm works in a two-step
process. In the prediction step, the Kalman filter produced the estimates of the current state variables, along with their uncertainties.
Once the outcome of the next measurement observed, these estimates are updated. It handled the unpredictability due to the noisy
data. We used the Kalman filter to smooth the multivariate time series sequences and stored the data in a .csv file format for further
use.

6. Conclusion and future work

    In this work, we presented real-time HCI framework for recognizing the emotion of a human by using the multivariate time-
series thermal video sequences. This research contribution incorporates three stages. In stage-I, we proposed that Faster R-CNN
architecture provides 99% accuracy in our developed data-set and NVIE data-set. The detected facial ROIs were tracked across the
frames by using the MIL algorithm, and the mean intensity was found out which represents the multivariate time series (MTS)
sequences. The classification of MTS data is done by measuring how similar two MTS data sequences are. The Kalman filtering
process is used to smoothing the MTS sequences data. The DTW algorithm is used for similarity measurement of two MTS sequences
and to classify the emotion with accuracy of 64.07% and 70.59% in both non-smoothed and smoothed data. We observed that the
average classification accuracy yielded higher when compared to the other start-of-art methods in stage-II. Our designed, customized
python module suggested some relevant physical and psychological distraction techniques through a pop-up window in stage-III.
The proposed system is useful in many psychological applications, useful for automated counseling, as an aid to psychologists.
    This work can be extended in the further directions. The classification accuracy can improve by using other time series analysis
algorithms. The clustering of sequences for recognition stress, anxiety, and depression levels in real-time HCI framework is not the
scope of this work, which may focus on our future work. A critical restriction of this study is the limited number of subjects, which
restricts to illustrate a generalized inference about human emotion. A more significant number of participants is desirable for future
study, with interpersonal variations in psychological health disorders.
