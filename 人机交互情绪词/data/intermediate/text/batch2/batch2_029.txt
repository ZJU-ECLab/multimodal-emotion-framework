Designing towards emotional usability in customer interfaces-
      trustworthiness of cyber-banking system interfaces

                                       Jinwoo Kim*, Jae Yun Moon


           Received   I February 1997; received in revised form I May 1997: accepted I November 1997




Abstract

   In this research we investigate the possibility of designing a user interface for electronic commerce
systems that will evoke target feelings in the customer. The focus is on the impact of visual design
factors on the feeling of trustworthiness       because of its significant effect upon the behavior of
customers using electronic commerce systems. Four empirical studies were conducted in the domain
of cyber-banking systems. The subjects were cyber-banking system developers, bank personnel and
potential customers of cyber-banking systems in Korea ranging in age from late teens to early forties.
The first study was directed at developing the self-report questionnaire that faithfully reflects the
emotional factors related to cyber-banking systems. The resulting questionnaire consisted of the
forty bipolar emotive differential scales representative of the emotions most important in interacting
with cyber-banking systems, e.g. reliable-not reliable. The second study focused on determining the
important visual design factors from the customer’s perspective. Fourteen design factors identiticd
from subjects’ descriptions were classified into the four design categories of title, menu. main clipurt
and color. The third study investigated the correlations between the emotional factors and design
factors. The design factors were found to have significant effects upon the extent of feelings related
to symmetry, trustworthiness. awkwardness and elegance. In the final study. two interfaces were
designed based on the results of the third study to differentiate the extent of trustworthiness evoked.
The results indicate that it is possible to manipulate the visual design factors of the customer inter-
face in order to induce a target emotion, such as trustworthiness. The paper concludes by discussing
the implications of the empirical results on the design and implementation of customer interfaces to
electronic commerce systems in general. 0 1998 Elsevier Science B.V.

1. Introduction

   The importance of subjective feeling or emotion has been analyzed in numerous studies

[1,2], including several on the role of emotions in cognitive processes [3,4]. Prior studies
found that emotions play an important role in problem solving [5] and decision making
[6,7] by providing information on the emotional desirability of the options available,
thereby reducing and limiting reasoning to only those that induce positive feelings.
   The importance of emotion has also been emphasized in the design of physical products
[8,9, lo]. Logan et al. [ 111 maintained that emotional elements should be considered in the
design of consumer product categories such as pagers and automobiles because these are
the products that are used on a regular basis by the end users, thereby having a profound
impact on their overall emotional well being [S]. In order to achieve this goal, Lee and
Nagamachi [9] proposed a design methodology that has been used to develop various
physical products ranging from sports cars to coffee pots.
   Emotion should play as important a role in the design of computer interfaces as in that of
physical products [7,12]. People not only regard the computer as a medium for interaction
with other human beings, but also respond directly to the computer itself. In other words,
people behave as if the computer were a social actor, even though they know that the
machine does not actually possess human feelings [ 131. For example, it is not unusual to
hear someone yelling at a computer as if it might feel sorry and change its behavior [ 141.
The feelings that are aroused in interacting with a system are especially important for
systems that are used on a discretionary           basis such as home computing, games and
electronic commerce [15]. This is because people do not have to use such a system if
they feel bad about it.
   Electronic commerce can be defined as the delivery of information, products/services,
or payments via telephone lines, computer networks or any other electronic means [ 161. It
includes a wide range of applications such as electronic document interchange, cyber
shopping and cyber banking [17]. In this paper, we use the term ‘customer interface’ to
refer to the user interface of such electronic commerce systems [ 181. The purpose is to
shift the perspective from the user of a computer system to the customer of commercial
transactions. In real world commerce, the emotions and impressions evoked by the sales
agent or commercial institution as a whole influence the overall satisfaction of the cus-
tomer. In particular, the emotion of trustworthiness has been emphasized as one of the
most important factors for the successful completion of commercial transactions [17,19].
We purport that a similar phenomenon could occur in the context of interaction with
electronic commerce systems, where emotions evoked by the customer interface may
affect the overall satisfaction of the user and in particular the feeling of security in
conducting financial transaction with the particular system.
   However, little research has been conducted regarding the emotional usability of the
user interface in general, let alone the customer interface of electronic commerce systems.
Prior research on human-computer         interaction has focused on the cognitive usability of
the system. The issues of most interest were those of ease of use, efficiency, learnability
and error handling. These cognitive factors are definitely of extreme importance in
human-computer       interaction. However, the feelings of users interacting with the compu-
ter system are of equal importance because emotion interacts with cognition to achieve a

be, people will not use the system as intended if they feel insecure about the reliability of

   The main objective of this research is to formulate and evaluate a systematic methodol-
ogy for the design of customer interfaces that explicitly aim to generate certain target
feelings in the customer while interacting with electronic commerce systems. This paper
especially focuses on the feeling of trustworthiness that the cyber-banking    system inter-

the causal relationships between the two factors.



2. Emotions

   The concept of emotional usability derives in part from the following concepts of
emotions and their functions. After a brief overview of the nature and functions of
emotions, emotion elicitation procedures will be discussed. Finally, the importance and function
of the emotion of trust will be discussed in the context of human-computer          interaction.

2.1. De$nition   of emotion

   It is hard to define emotion precisely [14], but it can be considered as a multifaceted
phenomenon which encompasses a diversity of processes such as appraisal, facial expres-
sions. bodily responses, feeling states, action tendencies and coping strategies [2Ol. There
are two views of emotion: the basic category view, which maintains that emotions are
constituted of a fixed set of discrete categories [20,21], and the multidimensional     view
which proposes that emotions are differentiated along no more than two to four bipolar
dimensions     to constitute  an emotion space [22,23.24,25,26].       The basic rationale
behind the design of emotion-generating        interfaces is based on the multidimensional
view, which was used successfully as a basis of design methodologies            for physical
products [10,9].
   The emotion space is defined as a region of some unknown dimensionality            that is
determined by the nature of the situation to which the person is exposed [22]. The emotion
space can be constructed in terms of the major emotional dimensions, represented by
emotional scales. Each emotional scale, defined by a pair of polar (opposite in meaning)
adjectives, such as happy-not happy, is assumed to represent a straight line function that
passes through the origin of this space. To define the emotion space with maximum
efficiency. the minimum number of orthogonal dimensions or scales which exhausts the
dimensionality   of the space needs to be determined.

emotions of joy, sadness, amusement, interest, relief, pleasure, or anger [27], but the
nonbasic, domain-specific  emotions derived from these basic emotions [28]. These non-

2.2. Function of emotion

    Although rational thought and emotion are often depicted as being locked in an eternal
battle, there is typically more cooperation than strife [6]. Recent research into the nature of
emotion shows ample evidence with regard to this proposition. Neurobiological studies of
frontal lobe patients show that practical and social decision making is closely related to the
region of the brain connecting the emotional and cognitive centers [29]. Relying solely on
the cognitive resources results in a never-ending          cost-benefit   analysis of numerous
options, impeding the decision maker from coming to a practicable conclusion. Emotions
provide information on the emotional desirability of the options available and thereby
reduce and limit reasoning to those that induce positive feelings [6,30].
   It is especially important to take the informative function of emotions into account when
designing human-computer         interfaces. The interface may elicit a variety of emotions
ranging from the basic affective feelings such as joy or fear, to nonbasic feelings such
as trustworthiness or sophistication. Studies indicate that the various types of feelings all
influence judgment and decision making in a similar manner [30]. Therefore, the feelings
evoked by the human-computer         interface can also affect the quality of decision making in
using the computer system, For example, the emotions induced from the customer inter-
faces can enhance the quality of the customers’ experience by triggering informative
emotions that can aid decision making in using electronic commerce systems.


2.3. Emotion elicitation procedures

    Scientific research of emotion depends on the reliability of the procedures of emotion
elicitation used in the laboratory. Research on the elicitation of emotions under laboratory
conditions indicate that stimuli can be constructed to elicit certain target emotions [21]. A
large variety of emotion eliciting procedures such as films, slides and role playing were
used in these experiments [21,20].
    Of the diverse procedures available, films were found to be the most effective in
eliciting universally common target emotions among different subjects in the laboratory
[21]. This was attributed to the fact that films have a relatively high degree of ecological
validity, in so far as emotions are often evoked by dynamic visual and auditory stimuli that
are external to the individual [ZO]. Computer interfaces can basically be considered as a
collection of snap shots that are composed of diverse visual and auditory stimuli designed
to directly engage the user into the interaction [31]. Accordingly, human-computer      inter-
faces also have the potential to elicit universally common emotions in their users, justify-
ing the attempt to design the customer interface to elicit certain target emotions.

2.4. Emotion measurement       techniques

   Research about the nature of emotions is not possible without the means to measure the




                                                                                               Downloaded from academic.oup.com/iwc/article/10/1/1/912667 by Zhejiang University Library xixi campus user on 25 September 2026
elicited emotions. Emotions are displayed in a person’s facial expression and voice tone
and cause certain motor behaviors and physiological responses. Among the many avail-
able measurement techniques, self-reports are most commonly used as the measures of
emotion under laboratory conditions because of the ease of data collection [2 1.32,30].
   There are three types of self-report measures of emotions [20]. the Differential
Emotions Scale (DES), the Semantic Differential (SD) scale [24] and the free labeling
method. The DES measures the intensity of an elicited emotion, whereas the SD scale
pairs polar emotion terms and measures the intensity and direction of an emotion. Free
labeling methods require that subjects simply jot down what they are feeling at the time of
the experiment. Of the three, this research employed the SD scale for its association with
the multidimensional   approach to emotions, the view adopted in the design of emotion-
generating interfaces. The SD scale, based on the multidimensional    view of emotions, was
used successfully as the basis of applying kansei engineering to the design of such physical
products such as beepers and automobiles [9.10].

2.5. Trust in human-computer         interaction

   An important emotion in the domain of human-computer        interaction is the feeling of
trust that a user places in the computer system. Research on expert systems and other
computer systems in general suggests that the perceived trustworthiness of the computer
system has a significant impact on the way people use it [33]. For example, Woods et al.
1341 found that when technicians do not trust a decision aid, they may either reject its
advice or attempt to manipulate the output of the decision aid toward their own precon-
ceived solutions.
   Trustworthiness   is of even more importance in the customer interface of electronic
commerce systems due to its close relationship to marketing and promotion [ 191. In
marketing, factors such as the communicator’s     physical appearance were found to have
a considerable influence on the feeling of trustworthiness   and the tinal decision to buy
[35.36.37,38.39]. In the case of an electronic commerce system, the user interacts with the
system via the customer interface, which can be considered as the physical appearance of
the electronic commerce systems. Therefore, the interface of electronic commerce systems
must be designed so as to induce trustworthy feelings within the customer, which in turn
will influence the decisions to use the system.
   This research places an emphasis upon the visual stimuli of a customer interface to
electronic commerce systems and purports to determine how the visual aspect can influ-
ence the extent of trustworthiness evoked by the customer interface.

3.1. Study 1

   The purpose of the first study was to develop the self-report questionnaire to be used for
measuring the emotions elicited by cyber-banking        interfaces. The resulting questionnaire
consisted of the differential scales that best represent the dimensions of the emotion space
for cyber-banking    systems.

3.1.1. Materials

3.1.1.1. Questionnaire. Since the purpose of this first study was to select the differential
scales that are appropriate for emotion measurement, a comprehensive search was made of
the relevant pools of emotion terms. A self-report questionnaire developed by Lee (Self-
report questionnaire, unpublished research material) as part of an empirical study of the
kansei engineering methodology [9,10], served as the base set of emotive differential
scales. Additional terms that would be relevant in the context of cyber-banking           system
interfaces were selected from the following sources. A survey of employees and customers
of a financial institution was conducted in which they were asked to freely label the
emotions that they associated with cyber-banking              systems. Emotion terms were also
gathered from pamphlets of cyber-banking             services and research papers about cyber-
banking systems [17]. Finally, dictionaries and thesauruses were consulted to include
multiple terms for the same emotion or feeling. This was done to ensure that all
possible modes of expressing             a certain emotion         were provided  in the initial
questionnaire.    For example, the emotion of happiness can be expressed through the
terms, ‘pleased, overjoyed, contented, satisfied’ and many other terms. Each of these
terms is appropriate in a particular situation and not in others. It is therefore necessary
to include all possible terms that express the same basic emotion since it is not known a
priori which is most suitable for cyber-banking            systems. Determining  which of these
multiple terms is most relevant in the context of interacting with cyber-banking         systems
is the goal of this first study. A total of 3 18 terms were selected and arranged in the form of
bipolar seven-point Likert scales, a sample of which is shown in Fig. 1. The questionnaire
used in the studies was formulated in the Korean language, the native language of the
subjects in the study, so as to enable the accurate measurement of the subtle differences in
the emotional terms. However, in order to present readers with a general understanding of
the procedures undertaken in our study, we will translate the emotion terms used in the
questionnaire to the closest term in the English language.

eight interfaces were selected as the experiment material. Special care was taken in the

3.1.2.2. Procedure. The subjects were given the standard instructions            for semantic
differential   questionnaires    and were especially       cautioned   to mark their ‘first

had understood the nature of the task, subjects were shown one slide at a time and asked to

scales. The slides were shown on a 43 inch color monitor. The order of the interfaces was
counter-balanced    for each of the three group sessions and subjects were given one of five

version of the questionnaire presented the emotive scales in random order so that no two
versions were the same. The subjects were given 5 min after each slide to relax and empty
their minds of the previous slide in order to avoid carrying over the feelings from the
previous interface to the evaluation of the next.

3.1.3. Results and discussion
   Each slot on the seven-point scale was assigned a number from 1 to 7 starting from the
left. A total of 200 self-questionnaires   (8 slides X 25 subjects), each consisting of 318
scales were coded according to the assigned numbers. The variables in this data set were
the 318 emotive differential scales.
    Since the important emotional factors related to the interfaces of cyber-banking systems
is not known a priori, cluster analysis was used to reduce the redundant scales through the
grouping of closely related ones. A total of 15 clusters were determined through complete
cluster analysis, of which five were discarded due to significant discrepancies with the
results of Wards cluster analysis.’ A few emotive differential scales that were most
appropriate for measuring the feelings elicited by cyber-banking       system interfaces were
selected from each of the remaining 10 clusters. The initial selection of the emotive scales
to be included in the final set was done on the basis of the communality values of the terms
computed as a result of a maximum likelihood factor analysis. The final set was deter-
mined by a group of domain experts consisting of two cyber-banking           system designers
and two general cyber-banking       domain experts, who were presumed to have a richer


    ‘Cluster analysis was deemed to be the most appropriate method in reducing the number of emotive scales; the
resulting clusters would exhibit high internal (within-cluster) homogeneity and high external (between-cluster)
heterogeneity, thereby enabling the selection of the minimum required scales that would measure all necessary
aspects of the emotions evoked by the cyber-banking       system interfaces. However, cluster analysis is a highly
empirical method in which different methods can lead to very different groupings, both in number and in content
[40]. Therefore, we used two methods to validate the selection of the clusters and the representative emotive
scales within those clusters. The two methods were Ward’s cluster analysis and complete cluster analysis. In
complete cluster analysis, the distance between two clusters is the maximum distance between an observation in
one cluster and an observation in the other cluster. In Ward’s minimum-variance   method, the distance betwen two
clusters is the ANOVA sum of squares between the two clusters added up over all the variables. The final decision
to discard a particular cluster, however, was made after a thorough discussion with cyber-banking system experts.

   Prior to analyzing the drawings made by the subjects, a group of cyber-banking system

interfaces. The group of cyber-banking     system designers then analyzed the 236 drawings
of the interfaces in order to incorporate the customers’ viewpoint. Fig. 3 shows a sample of

   The drawings were closely analyzed by interface designers of cyber-banking systems in

incorporated in the interface (e.g. figures of people and the interior of the bank in
Fig. 3). Common features noticed by a majority of the subjects for each interface were
labeled as design factors based on the initial classification scheme developed.

3.3. Study 3

   The third study had two purposes: first to determine the major dimensions of the
emotion space of customers interacting with cyber-banking    systems, and second to estab-
lish the causal relationships between the emotive dimensions and design factors of a cyber-
banking interface.

4. Conclusion     and discussion

   Electronic commerce systems may replace the traditional institutions of today as the
preferred medium of commercial transactions [44]. Many firms have already implemented
some form of home shopping system in order to reap the benefits of electronic commerce,
namely lower costs, customer service enhancement and reduced time to market. However,
the key to the success of electronic commerce systems lies in a wide adoption of such
technologies by the customer [45]. It is therefore essential to attract the casual navigator on
the World Wide Web, a potential customer of any electronic commerce system. Whether
or not the casual navigator will become a loyal customer of a particular cyber shopping
mall or cyber bank may depend on the first impression of the interface. Therefore, the
emotions aroused by the interface of electronic commerce systems must be systematically
incorporated into the design of customer interfaces.
   The central question addressed by this paper was that of the possibility of designing
customer interfaces which can involve the users emotionally and thus enhance the quality
of the decisions made while interacting with electronic commerce systems. However, the
design of interfaces that evoke positive affect in the customers is important primarily
because such interfaces will encourage the customer to return to the particular site, a
matter of supreme importance in electronic commerce. The results indicate that it is
possible to design customer interfaces of cyber-banking        systems that will elicit target
emotions, in particular trustworthiness.    Moreover, there was little individual variation
in the quality of emotion brought forth by the cyber-banking            interfaces used in the
research.
   However, the results of this paper are not immediately applicable to the actual design of
customer interfaces because of the following limitations. First, subjects in this research
were asked to indicate the immediate feelings evoked by a single slide of each cyber-
banking system interface. Therefore, the emotions indicated were the result of passive
exposure to the visual interface, not the actual usage of the cyber-banking        system. Per-
forming financial transactions, on the contrary, is not simply a mechanical process of

research could not perform a comprehensive         analysis of interaction effects, it could not
fully account for emergent or ‘gestalt’ interface features that depend on the entire set of
design factors rather than individual factors. It is left to future studies to determine the
complex interrelations between the design factors and emotional factors, a task requiring a
modified classification scheme of design factors and an alternative technique for measur-
ing emotions.
    The third limitation came from the participants in our empirical studies. Even though
their age, sex and occupations were varied in order to account for the diverse customers in
electronic commerce, they were homogeneous with respect to their socio-cultural back-
grounds. i.e. ethnography and nationality. It is hard to assume that people with different
sociocultural backgrounds would feel the same emotion to the interfaces. Future studies
should consider the difference of emotional factors resulting from diverse cultures and
 nations, which is important in building electronic commerce systems that has international
usability [47].
    The final limitation of this research stems from the assumptions on which the experi-
mental procedures and analyses were based. The research made the assumption that
emotions elicited by a particular customer interface are determined by those design
features that command the customer’s attention, a proposition based on theories in
human cognition and emotion [7,8,24,31,34].            The second assumption        made in the
research was that the factor structure determined in the third study would be preserved
 for the fourth study due to similarities in the composition of the subject pool and experi-
mental task. A third assumption underlying the experimental method employed relates
directly to the operational definition of the emotional constructs and the measurement tool
 used in the studies. The self-report questionnaires of the forty emotive differential scales
 were used as the sole indicators of the emotions elicited by the customer interfaces and
 were not complemented        by such measures of emotions as facial expressions or action
 tendency protocols. This was done under the assumption that subjects’ subjective feelings
 can be faithfully measured by self-report scales. Another assumption that was made in the
 process of developing the self-report questionnaire        was that cyber-banking     designers
 would have a richer vocabulary for describing the emotive aspects of banking systems.
 Finally, the subject pool used in the study consisted of those users accustomed to the
 Internet and online transactions, who in turn would be more liable to deem a particular
22                  J. Kim, J. Y. Moon/Interacting with Computers 10 (1998) l-29

cyber-banking system interface to be reliable than elderly people. Future studies should be
conducted on different subject pools to verify the validity of such assumptions and search
for other experimental methods that do not require such assumptions.

   Despite these limitations, the results of this research have interesting implications for
the future of human-computer       interaction, especially in the area of electronic commerce
systems. They demonstrate that it is possible to elicit target emotions in a majority of
subjects, thus making it possible to design emotive interfaces for widespread use. Further-
more, the possibility of generalizing        the findings to include all electronic commerce
system interfaces was raised by a similar research that was conducted with a different
system of electronic commerce (e.g. cyber shopping malls). Preliminary results from the
research reveal that similar-emotive and design factors can be identified in cyber shopping
malls, an indication that it is possible to develop common emotive and design factors for
electronic commerce systems. Furthermore, connections can be established between the
physical design factors and emotions elicited by the interface, thereby enabling an explicit
method of dealing with the emotional usability of customer interfaces. Interfaces thus
designed will be able to appeal to the individual customer’s emotions and make the
experience of interacting with the computer system, whether it be to shop, to deposit
money in a bank account, or to play a game, an emotionally engaging experience, a
mimesis of the actual world [31].

Appendix A. Structure of the emotion space

   In study 3, maximum likelihood factor analysis using promax rotation was performed
on 1417 observations (12 interfaces X 123 respondents, with 59 observations eliminated
because of missing values). The variables were the 40 emotive differential scales shown in
Table 1. In the analysis, interfaces were treated as subjects, resulting in a mix of dependent
and independent cases. It would, however, have been statistically correct to have treated
the responses of each subject for the 12 interfaces as the 480 variables on which to perform
the factor analysis, the analysis was performed as such for the following reasons. First, the
objective of the third study was in determining the interrelation between the design factors
of cyber-banking    system interfaces and the emotional factors. The focus was not on the
interfaces themselves, but rather on the diverse design factors used in designing those
visual interfaces. Therefore, it was necessary to obtain the normalized factor scores for
each separate interface in order to determine the role that each design feature played in
evoking the particular emotional reaction. Second, the purpose of the factor analysis was
in reducing the forty emotive variables into the major dimensions that are actually of

consequence in the interaction with cyber-banking      systems. However, if the factor analy-
sis were performed on the 480 variables (12 interfaces X 40 emotive scales). it would have
been impossible to interpret the results in terms of the major emotional dimensions. Third,

an examination of the sample covariance matrix shows that it is reasonably safe to pre-
sume that the subjects responses to the 40 emotive scales for one interface are not closely
interrelated with their responses for a second interface. A sample covariance matrix of the
following form was derived from the data for the third study.




The subjects’ responses to the 12 interfaces were all treated as variables. resulting in 480
variables. Hence, the sample covariance matrix has 480 rows and 480 columns. In order to
justify the analysis method employed in the third study it is necessary to show that the
sample covariance matrix is a block diagonal matrix. In other words. the 40 responses to a
single interface constitute a block, where the responses for interface 1 are highly correlated
with the responses to interface 1, but have little or no correlation to the responses to
 interfaces 2 through 12.
    A close examination of the sample covariance matrix revealed that although not a block
diagonal matrix, the covariances in the block diagonal were consistently higher than
nondiagonal areas. Therefore, the decision to treat the subjects’ responses to the 12 inter-
 faces as independent cases in the factor analysis was justifiable.
