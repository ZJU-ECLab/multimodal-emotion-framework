Recent research has documented the effects of emotional factors on multimedia learn-
ing (Mayer & Estrella, 2014; Plass, Heidig, Hayward, Homer, & Um, 2014; Schneider,
Dyrna, Meier, Beege, & Rey, 2018; Um, Plass, Hayward, & Homer, 2012, Uzun & Yıldırım,
2018). Learners experience a variety of emotions while learning (D’Mello, 2013; Pekrun,
Goetz, Titz, & Perry, 2002), and positive emotions can promote learning (Efklides,
Kourkoulou, Mitsiou, & Ziliaskopoulou, 2006; Pekrun, 2006). However, no research to
date has examined emotional factors in the development of learning materials.
Positive emotional design refers to creating learning materials with pleasing character-
istics that ideally promote positive emotion and in turn better learning performance. It
is unclear at this point whether the positive emotional design of multimedia learning
materials can induce learners’ positive emotions and facilitate learning. It is also
unclear whether the effects of positive emotional design would vary based on prior
knowledge in a particular domain; in general, the effects of learners’ prior knowledge
on multimedia learning still need to be investigated (Kalyuga, 2007; Kalyuga, Ayres,
Chandler, & Sweller, 2003). The present study continues this research trend and further
explored the effects of prior knowledge on emotional design in multimedia learning
with a different population. Specifically, this research differs from earlier research in
two respects. First, in contrast to earlier studies on college students, this study
focussed on middle school students’ multimedia learning. Second, this is the first
study to test whether the effects of emotional design on learning vary for students
with high vs. low prior knowledge of the learning topic.


Literature review
Learning with multimedia
Multimedia learning can be defined as learning from a combination of words and pic-
tures, and the use of multimedia materials and tools for instruction is widespread
(Mayer, 2009). Computer-based lessons that contain animation and illustration are a
common format in which multimedia learning takes place. The Cognitive Theory of
Multimedia Learning (CTML; Mayer, 2005) describes the process of how learners select,
organise and integrate information in the multimedia learning environment, with an
emphasis on the role of cognitive processes such as essential processing, extraneous
processing, and generative processing. A more recent perspective, the Cognitive
Affective Theory of Learning with Media (CATLM), adds an emphasis on emotion and
motivation in multimedia learning; for example, according to the CATLM, the affective
features of instructional messages can influence learners’ cognitive processing and
learning outcomes (Moreno, 2006; Moreno & Mayer, 2007). The CATLM extends the
CTML by articulating three new assumptions: (1) affective mediation: affective factors
that can increase or decrease learners’ cognitive engagement may affect learning; (2)
individual differences: learner characteristics, such as prior knowledge, may affect multi-
media learning; and (3) metacognitive mediation: metacognitive processes that regulate
cognition and emotions may affect learning (Moreno, 2007; Moreno & Mayer, 2007).
   Previous studies have shown inconsistent results concerning the effects of emo-
tional design features on cognitive processes and learning outcomes. On the one
hand, the less-is-more approach holds that fewer design features can minimise
1078     C. SHANGGUAN ET AL.


extraneous processing (Mayer, 2014). Indeed, adding seductive details (e.g. seductive
illustrations) in multimedia learning can increase cognitive load and hinder learning
(Gong, Duan, Wang, Zhou, & Lu, 2014, Park, Moreno, Seufert, & Br€      unken, 2011), a phe-
nomenon called the seductive detail effect (Harp & Mayer, 1997). On the other hand,
the more-is-more approach claims that adding design features will induce positive
emotions and motivate learners’ engagement (Mayer, 2014). Consistent with this possi-
bility, incorporating decorative pictures in multimedia materials has been shown to
induce situational interest (Park & Lim, 2007) and positive emotions (Lenzner, Schnotz,
& Mu €ller, 2013), and to be beneficial for learning (Schneider et al., 2018). Furthermore,
the focussed-more-is-more approach contends that adding design features that can
increase learners’ motivation while providing adequate guidance to learners can be
beneficial for learning. For example, Mayer and Estrella (2014) found that adding emo-
tionally appealing elements to multimedia learning materials and providing support
by allowing learners sufficient time to do their work induced learners’ positive emo-
tions and facilitated learning.
    Each of these approaches has evidence to support it. On the one hand, additional
design features can create cognitive overload; on the other hand, if the design fea-
tures induce positive emotion, they might promote learning. Our assumption is that
when additional design features are appealing and strongly connected to the learning
content, they may induce positive emotions and facilitate learning regardless of
whether they increase cognitive load.


Emotional design in multimedia learning
Positive emotional design refers to the use of different design features in multimedia
materials to increase learners’ positive emotions and thus enhance learning (Park,
Kn€orzer, Plass, & Br€
                     unken, 2015). In contrast to the common practice of adding decora-
tive pictures to multimedia learning materials, emotional design aims to use combina-
tions, shapes and anthropomorphism to the intrinsic learning content but adds no
additional information related to the learning topic (Brom, Starkova, & D’Mello, 2018;
Navratil, Ku€hl, & Heidig, 2018). Thus, emotional design can be seen as a new way of
infusing motivational factors into multimedia learning (Mayer & Estrella, 2014, Uzun &
Yıldırım, 2018).
   Experiments on emotional design in multimedia learning materials, conducted pri-
marily in samples of college students, have largely manipulated emotional design fea-
tures such as colour and anthropomorphism (Brom et al., 2018; Mayer & Estrella, 2014;
Park et al., 2015; Plass et al., 2014; Schneider, Nebel, & Rey, 2016; Um et al., 2012).
These studies have explored the effects of emotional design on both affective out-
comes (e.g. motivation, satisfaction and perceived achievement) and cognitive out-
comes (e.g. cognitive load, comprehension and transfer) but have yielded inconclusive
results. Many studies have found that positive emotional design in learning materials
can induce positive emotions and motivation (Gong, Shangguan, Zhai, & Guo, 2017;
Um et al., 2012) and decrease perceived difficulty (Plass et al., 2014; Um et al., 2012)
while facilitating recall (Uzun & Yıldırım, 2018), comprehension (Mayer & Estrella, 2014;
Plass et al., 2014; Um et al., 2012) and transfer (Gong et al., 2017; Um et al., 2012).
                                                           EDUCATIONAL PSYCHOLOGY       1079


For example, Um et al. (2012) used the principles of emotional design to modify learn-
ing materials on the topic of “how immunization works.” Whereas the old learning
materials were presented in monochromatic greyscale featuring rectangular shapes,
the new materials were presented with colourful round shapes in a Flash animation
and HTML. They found that this positive emotional design had positive effects on cog-
nitive load and learning performance. However, other studies have found no effect of
emotional design on positive emotions and motivation; in these studies, positive
design failed to decrease cognitive load, let alone promote learning performance
(Heidig, M€ uller, & Reichelt, 2015; Park et al., 2015).
   There are several possible reasons for these inconsistent findings across studies. First,
in studies with null results the emotional design elements may not have been sufficient
to produce change. Consistent with this possibility, Plass et al. (2014) found that the use
of only warm colours did not induce positive emotions, but the combination of warm
colours and shape design did induce positive emotions. Second, anthropomorphic fea-
tures in learning materials can improve learners’ affective outcomes and motivation
(Schneider, Nebel, Beege, & Rey, 2017), as well as learning performance (Brom,
Hannemann, Starkova, Bromova, & Dechterenko, 2016; Schneider et al., 2017), but the
degree of anthropomorphism varies across designs. For example, Park et al. (2015) found
that a circular feature similar to a baby’s face did not arouse learners’ positive emotions,
whereas Mayer and Estrella (2014) found that a combination of circular, expressive and
anthropomorphic features did promote multimedia learning. Third, previous studies have
neglected to take into account learners’ individual differences (e.g. Gong et al., 2017; Um
et al., 2012). Both the CTML and the CATLM maintain that learners’ prior knowledge plays
an important role in integrating new information into long-term memory.
   Based on previous research, the present study combined colour and anthropo-
morphic design elements to investigate the effect of emotional design on both cogni-
tive and affective outcomes in middle school students. The colour design was not
limited to the contrast between cool and warm colours but also included objects’ nat-
ural colours. The anthropomorphic elements relied not only on shape but also expres-
sion. Natural colours and expressive anthropomorphism can be added to the visual
interface of most learning resources without adding learning content.


Learners’ prior knowledge in multimedia learning
Learners’ prior knowledge is conceptualised in terms of existing cognitive structures or
knowledge structures relevant to the domain being studied (Shing & Brod, 2016). It is
regarded as one of the most important individual factors that influence multimedia
learning outcomes (Kalyuga, Chandler, & Sweller, 1998; Park et al., 2015). Previous
research indicated that learners’ prior knowledge affects learning processes and out-
comes (Korbach, Bru €nken, & Park, 2017; Park et al., 2015). Specifically, prior knowledge
can help learners focus their attention on the central concepts and principles of learn-
ing materials and help them integrate new concepts and principles into existing cog-
nitive structures (Wetzels, Kester, & Merrienboer, 2011). When presented with new
learning material, learners with high prior knowledge can search for relevant informa-
tion quickly because they have already stored the relevant knowledge structure in
1080      C. SHANGGUAN ET AL.


long-term memory (Charness, Reingold, Pomplun, & Stampe, 2001). By contrast, learn-
ers with low prior knowledge require more cognitive resources, and cognitive load
may exceed the capacity of working memory. Cognitive overload, in turn, exerts a
negative influence on motivation (Kalyuga, 2007; Paas, Tuovinen, Merri€enboer, &
Darabi, 2005). Together, these pieces of evidence favour the assumption that learners
with high prior knowledge will benefit more from the learning environment than
learners with low prior knowledge will.
   Multimedia learning materials require learners need to actively process information
(Mayer, 2005; Moreno, 2006; Moreno & Mayer, 2007). Prior knowledge may influence how
learners perceive the visual appeal or aesthetics of the learning material and these percep-
tions, in turn, may affect engagement and learning outcomes. Specifically, learners with
low prior knowledge may feel more interested when aesthetic elements are incorporated
into the learning material, as emotional design can help present abstract and complex
principles more vividly (e.g. Mayer & Estrella, 2014), thus facilitating learning. By contrast,
learners with high prior knowledge are able to integrate information into larger units and
may already have developed certain automatic processes to decrease their working mem-
ory load (Magner, Schwonke, Aleven, Popescu, & Renkl, 2014; Sweller, 2005). Therefore,
they may be less influenced by emotional design. It could thus be inferred that compared
to neutral emotional design, positive emotional design might be more beneficial for learn-
ers with low prior knowledge than for those with high prior knowledge.
   Although learners’ prior knowledge might moderate the effect of emotional design
on multimedia learning, little empirical research has directly investigated the inter-
action between emotional design and learners’ prior knowledge (Brom et al., 2018).
Thus, taking individual differences, especially learners’ prior knowledge, into consider-
ation in the multimedia learning environment may be beneficial as a test of the indi-
vidual differences assumption of the CATLM (Moreno, 2006).


Goal and hypotheses of the study
This study aimed to test the effect of positive emotional design on learning in middle
school students, and whether the effect is moderated by learners’ prior knowledge.
Based on the CATLM (Moreno, 2006; Moreno & Mayer, 2007) and the above literature
review, we formed the following hypotheses:
   Hypothesis 1: Compared with neutral emotional design, positive emotional design will
   induce and maintain positive emotions and facilitate motivational outcomes (higher
   motivation, perceived achievement, and satisfaction) and cognitive outcomes (lower
   cognitive load, higher retention and transfer scores).
   Hypothesis 2: Compared with learners with low prior knowledge, learners with high prior
   knowledge will have better cognitive outcomes (lower perceived difficulty, higher mental
   effort, retention, and transfer scores) and better motivational outcomes (higher motivation
   and satisfaction).
   Hypothesis 3: Learners with low prior knowledge will have better cognitive outcomes
   (lower perceived difficulty, higher mental effort, retention, and transfer scores) and better
   motivational outcomes (higher motivation and satisfaction) in positive emotional design
   conditions than in neutral conditions, whereas learners with high prior knowledge may be
   less influenced by emotional design.

Method
Participants and design
A paper-and-pencil prior knowledge test about the learning topic “the formation of
lightning” was administered a week before the formal study to select the participants.
Participants were recruited from middle schools in China. The top 27% of students,
based on the prior knowledge test score, were classified as the high prior knowledge
group, and the bottom 27% were classified as the low prior knowledge group. The
final participants were 124 middle school students (66 male and 58 female; age:
M ¼ 14.40, SD ¼ 0.75), of whom sixty-three participants were high prior knowledge
learners and sixty-one were low prior knowledge learners. Descriptive information
about the high and low prior knowledge groups is presented in Table 1. Participants
had normal or corrected-to-normal vision. The study protocol was approved by the
Ethics Committee of the School of Psychology at . Permission for the study was
obtained from school authorities and principals. Written informed consent was also
obtained from parents prior to the experiment.
   A 2  2 design was used in this study with two factors: learners’ prior knowledge
(high vs. low) and emotional design (positive vs. neutral). Participants in the high and
low knowledge groups were randomly assigned to one of the two emotional design
groups. Therefore, there were four conditions: high prior knowledge and positive emo-
tional design group (HKPE, n ¼ 33), high prior knowledge and neutral emotional
design group (HKNE, n ¼ 30), low prior knowledge and positive emotional design
group (LKPE, n ¼ 30), and low prior knowledge and neutral emotional design group
(LKNE, n ¼ 31).


Materials
Design of the learning materials
A computer-based lesson covering the topic “the formation of lightning” was used as
the learning material (Mayer, 2009; Gong et al., 2017), which was presented as a Flash

animation that lasted 152 s. Two different versions of emotional design were imple-
mented. The positive emotional design (PE) applied colour and anthropomorphism to
the cloud, the positive and negative charges, etc. We made the colour of the objects
in the learning material to be as similar as possible to the objects’ real colour. In add-

Measures
Emotional state
Positive emotional state was measured before and after the computer-based lesson.
Six items from the Positive Emotion Self-Report Inventory (Gross & Levenson, 1995)
were used to measure positive emotions (happy, excited, content, active, interested
and relaxed). These items have been successfully applied in previous studies (e.g.
Chaffar & Frasson, 2004; Gong et al., 2017). Participants were asked to describe to
what extent they felt various emotions about the learning in general on a 9-point
Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 9 (very much). The positive emotion score
was obtained by averaging the scores for the 6 responses. This scale had high internal
consistency in the current study (coefficient a ¼ .90 pre-lesson; .91 post-lesson).

Prior knowledge about the learning topic
Prior knowledge about the learning topic “the formation of lightning” was assessed a
week before the formal experiment, using a 4-item self-report checklist (e.g. “I know
how clouds are formed”) ranging from 0 (I have no idea at all) to 4 (I totally under-
stand), 5 multiple-choice questions (e.g. “How does a cloud form? A. Air flow rises; B.
Temperature decreases; C. Water vapour condenses into water droplets; D. Do not
know”) and an open question (“Please write down everything you know about the for-
mation of lightning”) (see Supplementary Appendix). Each learner’s prior knowledge
score was calculated by summing the points from all 10 items (self-rating scores of 0
to 4 on four items, maximum 16 points; 1 point for each correct answer on five mul-
tiple-choice questions, maximum 5 points; 1 point for each key point mentioned in
                                                             EDUCATIONAL PSYCHOLOGY        1083


response to the open question, maximum 10 points), resulting in a range from 0 to 31
points. All the items had been successfully used in previous studies (Harp & Mayer,
1997; Gong et al., 2014; Gong et al., 2017).

Learning outcomes
Learning outcomes consisted of instant retention and transfer tests (see Supplementary
Appendix). The retention test was used to measure learners’ memory of the learning con-
tent and included 9 multiple-choice questions (e.g. “How does lightning form?”), with 1
point assigned for each correct answer. The transfer test was used to measure learners’
ability to transfer what they had learned to new problems (e.g. “Explain why sometimes
there are clouds but no lightning in the sky”), with 1 point assigned for each idea repre-
senting a key point (maximum 10 points) mentioned in response to three open questions.
The total transfer score was obtained by summing all responses, ranging from 0 to 30
points. The transfer test was rated by two raters, with an inter-rater reliability of r ¼ 0.95.

Additional dependent measures
Cognitive Load. Two items were used to assess each learner’s cognitive load. One item
measured perception of task difficulty (Kalyuga, Chandler, & Sweller, 2000): “How easy
or difficult was the material to understand?” (1 ¼ extremely easy, 9 ¼ extremely hard).
The other item measured mental effort (Paas, 1992): “How much mental effort did you
invest in studying the previous materials?” (1 ¼ none, 9 ¼ the most possible). Both
items were included in the cognitive load score, as they measure different constructs
(Deleeuw & Mayer, 2008) and have been successfully used in previous studies (Gong
et al., 2017; Park et al., 2015).
   Motivation. A 7-point Likert-type self-report instrument containing 7 items, devel-
oped by Isen and Reeve (2005), was used to measure motivation (e.g. “The material
aroused my curiosity,” 1 ¼ strongly disagree, 7 ¼ strongly agree). The scale has been
successfully used in previous studies (Gong et al., 2017; Um et al., 2012). The final
motivation score was calculated by averaging the scores for all responses (coeffi-
cient a ¼ .92).
   Satisfaction and perceived learning achievement. Learners rated their satisfaction
with the learning experience using the computer-based multimedia lesson using one
7-point Likert-type item: “How much did you like the learning material?” (1¼ extremely
disliked, 7¼ extremely liked). They rated their perceived learning achievement using a
7-point Likert-type item: “How well do you think you did in the preceding tests?”
(1 ¼ extremely poorly, 7 ¼ extremely well).

Control measures
The learning topic in this study was related to physics, and thus, interest in physics
and interest in the specific topic of lightning needed to be controlled. Learners’ inter-
est in physics was measured using one question: “How great is your interest in phys-
ics?” Learners’ interest in the topic was also assessed with one question: “How great is
your interest in the topic ‘the formation of lightning’ that you are going to learn
about?” Both items were Likert-type items that were rated on a 9-point scale (1 ¼ not
interested at all, 9 ¼ extremely interested).

Procedure
A week before the formal experiment, the participants were asked to complete the
prior knowledge test. Based on their scores on the test, 63 learners with high prior
knowledge (top 27% of all scores) and 61 learners with low prior knowledge (bottom
27% of all scores) were randomly assigned to the positive or the neutral emotional
design group. The formal experiment was administered through a computer pro-
gramme and paper-and-pencil questionnaires in a laboratory setting. The participants
were given a brief introduction to the present study, in which they would follow the
instructions on the computer to learn about “the principles of lightning formation.”
First, the participants completed the two measures on their interest in physics and
interest in the topic, as well as the pre-lesson inventory of positive emotion. Second,
the participants were presented with learning content on the screen in the form of
either positive or neutral emotional design features (based on their previously
assigned group). After learning the material, the participants were asked to complete
the positive emotion measure for the second time and then responded to questions
about cognitive load, motivation, retention and transfer, satisfaction with their learning
experience and perception of achievement. The entire process lasted approxi-
mately 45 min.


Results
Table 1 shows the descriptive statistics for all variables.


Preliminary analyses
First, to examine the difference in prior knowledge between the high and low prior
knowledge groups, an independent-samples t test was conducted, the results of which
showed a significant difference between the two groups, t(122) ¼ 21.65, p < .001,
d ¼ 3.89. The results revealed that grouping students into high and low prior know-
ledge groups was effective in the present study. Second, two 2  2 ANOVAs (emo-
tional design  prior knowledge) were conducted to check the difference between the
two prior knowledge groups on the two control variables (interest in physics and
interest in the topic). The results showed a main effect of prior knowledge on interest
in physics, F(1,120) ¼ 18.70, p < .001, g2p ¼ .14, and on interest in the topic, F(1,120)
¼18.76, p < .001, g2p ¼.14. Learners with high prior knowledge reported higher inter-
est in physics and higher interest in the topic. In the following analyses, the two con-
trol variables were used as covariates, with some exceptions (explained in the
next section).


Positive emotions
A preliminary step was to examine whether the initial emotional state was equivalent
across the four groups. A 2  2 ANOVA (emotional design  prior knowledge) showed
no significant main effect of emotional design and no significant interaction effect on
pre-lesson emotional state, Fs < 1.96, ps > .10. However, there was a significant main
                                                           EDUCATIONAL PSYCHOLOGY       1085


effect of learners’ prior knowledge, F(1,120) ¼15.84, p < .001, g2p ¼.12: the pre-lesson
emotional state of learners with high prior knowledge (6.69±.19) was significantly
more positive than that of learners with low prior knowledge (5.62±.19). To investigate
change in positive emotion across time, a 2  2 RM-ANOVA was conducted with learn-
ers’ prior knowledge and emotional design as the between-subject factors and posi-
tive emotion score as the repeated measure. Interest in physics and interest in the
topic were not included as covariates here (e.g. Park et al., 2015) because of their high
correlations with positive emotions. The results revealed a significant main effect of
learners’ prior knowledge, F(1,120) ¼ 21.99, p < .001, g2p ¼ .16: learners with high prior
knowledge reported higher positive emotions than those with low prior knowledge.
The main effect of time was significant, F(1,120) ¼ 23.39, p < .001, g2p ¼.16, indicating
an overall decrease in positive emotions from pre- to post-lesson. Contrary to expecta-
tions, no significant main effect of emotional design on positive emotions was shown,
F(1,120) ¼1.82, p > .05.


Learning outcomes
We conducted two 2  2 ANCOVAs with emotional design and prior knowledge as
between-subject factors, interest in physics and interest in the topic as covariates, and
retention and learning as the two dependent variables. The two covariates were
included because the high and low prior knowledge groups showed significant differ-
ences on these variables, and because the interactions between the independent vari-
ables (emotional design and learners’ prior knowledge) and the covariates (interest in
physics and interest in the topic) were not significant (Fs < 3.02, ps > .05); these results
met the assumption of homogeneity of regression slopes. In regard to the retention
test, analyses revealed no main effects of prior knowledge, F(1,118) ¼ .15, p > .10, no

Discussion
This study builds on previous research by optimising the positive emotional design
elements (colour and anthropomorphism) of computer-based learning materials to
investigate the effects of emotional design in a sample of middle school students. This
study also explored the moderating effect of learners’ prior knowledge on the effects
of emotional design on multimedia learning.
   Positive emotional design showed a trend towards facilitating learning transfer
(partly supporting Hypothesis 1). This finding was partly consistent with other evi-
dence that positive emotional design can be conducive to better learning outcomes
(Brom et al., 2018; Mayer & Estrella, 2014; Plass et al., 2014). Unexpectedly, the positive
emotional design in the present study failed to induce learners’ positive emotions (fail-
ing to support Hypothesis 1), including the positive emotions of learners with low
prior knowledge (failing to support Hypothesis 3). Thus, this study obtained no
1088     C. SHANGGUAN ET AL.


conclusive evidence that emotional design through colour and anthropomorphism
improves positive emotion. This null result is in line with a recent meta-analysis of 33
independent samples on emotional design (Brom et al., 2018). It can be inferred that
it may be difficult to induce positive emotions simply by adding visual design ele-
ments (colours and anthropomorphism) to learning material for middle school stu-
dents. One reason may be that simply including positive visual design elements is not
sufficient to induce positive emotions, as previous studies on emotional design have
indicated that more positive emotions are induced as the number of emotional design
elements increases (Uzun & Yıldırım, 2018). In addition, the learning content of the
present study may have been slightly difficult for middle school students, as the two
positive emotion measures showed a significant downward trend in all four groups of
learners. This decrease can be explained as motivation and positive affect decreasing
as a function of the learning material itself (Kn€  orzer, Br€
                                                             unken, & Park, 2016; Stark,
   €
Brunken, & Park, 2018).
    Meanwhile, learners with high prior knowledge tended to achieve better transfer
scores and to report higher mental effort than learners with low prior knowledge
(partly supporting Hypothesis 2). Analyses of the motivational outcomes revealed that
learners with high prior knowledge also were more satisfied than learners with low
prior knowledge (partly supporting Hypothesis 2). This result was consistent with the
findings of previous research (Korbach et al., 2017; Park et al., 2015) and can be inter-
preted as learners with high prior knowledge benefitting more from the multimedia
learning environment (Charness et al., 2001; Kalyuga, 2007). Learners with high prior
knowledge usually need less support and have more cognitive resources to process
new information than those with low prior knowledge. Moreover, it is also important
to point out that those learners with high prior knowledge reported higher positive
emotions before learning, which may be another reason that they reported higher sat-
isfaction. Further studies are needed to shed light on the relationship between posi-
tive emotions induced by emotional design and learners’ motivational outcomes.
    Contrary to our expectations, our data did not support the positive effects of emo-
tional design on the motivational outcomes (motivation, perceived achievement, and
satisfaction) or cognitive outcomes of learners with low prior knowledge (failing to
support Hypothesis 3). Our results were not consistent with those of previous
research on college students (Gong et al., 2017; Plass et al., 2014; Um et al., 2012).
The absence of an effect of emotional design on motivational outcomes for learners,
especially for those with low prior knowledge, was unexpected because positive
effects of emotional design on positive emotion, motivation, perceived difficulty and
transfer were observed in a previous study by Gong et al. (2017) using the same
learning topic and measures. One probable explanation is that learners with low prior
knowledge also have low self-efficacy about their ability to learn new material (Tsai,
Chuang, Liang, & Tsai, 2011), making it difficult for positive design features to induce
positive emotions and motivation. In addition, it should be pointed out that the par-
ticipants in the present study were middle school students. Compared with college
students, middle school students may have fewer cognitive resources. Applying emo-
tional design principles to learning material may therefore be distracting, especially in
the case of learners with low prior knowledge.
                                                         EDUCATIONAL PSYCHOLOGY     1089


   The present study found that emotional design displayed a trend towards improv-
ing mental effort for learners with low prior knowledge (partly supporting Hypothesis
3). This result was partly consistent with the findings of Mayer and Estrella
(Experiment 1; 2014). According to the CATLM, learners in the positive emotional
design group may produce higher mental effort which is regarded as an effective cog-
nitive load and is directly related to the task (van Merri€enboer & Ayres, 2005; Paas,
Renkl, & Sweller, 2004; Paas et al., 2005). Some studies have suggested that a higher
level of mental effort induces a deeper learning experience, which in turn supports
learning performance (Paas et al., 2005; Kalyuga, 2009), especially in learners with low
prior knowledge (Paas et al., 2005). Measurement issues might have contributed to
the finding that emotional design failed to induce learners’ positive emotions but
increased mental effort for learners with low prior knowledge. Specifically, self-
reported measures were used to assess emotions, and this approach may not be sensi-
tive enough to measure positive emotions pre- and post-lesson. In addition, learners
may not monitor their emotions continuously during learning because this process
consumes cognitive resources, especially for learners with low prior knowledge (Plass
& Kalyuga, 2019).


Implications
The present research contributes to the call to consider affective factors in a multi-
media learning environment (Mayer & Estrella, 2014). The results of the current study
are in line with the CATLM, which asserts that affective features in multimedia learning
can affect learners’ cognitive processing and learning outcomes. The results support
the more-is-more approach to some extent, as they show that emotional design pro-
motes transfer for all learners. In addition, our finding that learners’ prior knowledge
affects learning outcomes is consistent with the individual difference hypothesis of the
CATLM (Moreno, 2006). Our study also showed that emotional design had a tendency
to promote the mental effort of learners with low prior knowledge, a result with prac-
tical implications. For example, both emotional design and individual differences (such
as prior knowledge, motivation to learn and mental effort) could be taken into
account in instructional design. However, when emotional design principles are
applied in middle school settings, the material should be carefully designed to avoid
monopolising learners’ cognitive resources (Mctigue, 2009). Others have noted that
applying multimedia learning principles requires caution in this regard (Mayer, 2005;
Mayer, 2009).


Limitations and future directions
The present research has some limitations that should be considered. First, visual posi-
tive emotional design in the present study failed to induce learners’ positive emotions.
Other emotional design features, such as such as auditory emotional design and emo-
tional text design, should be tested as possible contributors to positive emotions and
learning (Norman, 2004; Uzun & Yıldırım, 2018). Hierarchical regression models can be
used to investigate the simultaneous effects of multiple positive design features.
1090      C. SHANGGUAN ET AL.


Second, the generalizability of the results must be tested in terms of learning content
and learning environment. This would involve testing the effects of positive emotional
design on learning a range of topics in real classroom learning environments. Finally,
additional individual differences can be tested as moderators of the effects of positive
emotional design on learning performance; these include working memory capacity,
executive functioning, and metacognition. The gender variable could also be included
and investigated in future research. Despite these limitations, our work meets the
need for evaluation of multimedia learning in middle school students and is consistent
with the call to consider affective factors in a multimedia learning environment.
