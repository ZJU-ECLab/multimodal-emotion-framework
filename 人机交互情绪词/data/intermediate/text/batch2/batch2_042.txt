1. Introduction
When Greek philosopher Plato claimed 2000 years ago that learning has an
emotional basis (Hinton, Miyamoto, & Della-Chiesa, 2008), people thought
that it was absurd. Throughout history, emotions have been negatively perceived
as harmful to the thought process and these pre-conceptions have been inﬂuen-
tial in the disparity of understanding the human behaviour. Then again, their
inﬂuence in all human activities, including the learning process, is undeniable

(Faria et al., 2017). Both positive and negative emotions have favourable and
unfavourable roles in the learning process and they may be deemed as “a
double-edged sword”. According to Moridis and Economides (2008), while posi-
tive emotions (joy, happiness, etc.) may broaden the thought process, it may also
distract students’ thinking. But, negative emotions (stress, anger, etc.) while we
assume may hinder the thought process may well help focus the mind while
learning. Equally, students may also experience a wide range of positive,
neutral or negative emotions that may or may not improve their learning experi-
ence (Lehman & Zapata-Rivera, 2018); henceforth, it is important to understand
the inﬂuence of these emotions so that it positively aﬀects learning experience.
As a result, Faria et al. (2017) stipulate that it is instrumental in understanding
how emotions inﬂuence learning.
   To date, emotions have been synonymously linked to terms such as feelings,
moods and aﬀects. Subjectively, this has been the basis for the numerous classiﬁ-
cation methods ranging from either aspects of dominance (weak or strong)
(Yan, Bracewell, & Ren, 2008), emotional basis (cognitive, evolutionary, physio-
logical and social constructivist) (Tsonos & Kouroupetroglou, 2007), activation,
valance, (Yan et al., 2008) and basic emotions (Izard, 2009). Hence, one of the
main issues in emotion research is the subjectivity and complexity of deﬁning
and categorising it. In education-based studies, valence, which is the positive
or negative nature of an emotion (Colombetti, 2005), has been widely used to
classify emotions (Park, 2004), similarly in e-learning (Page & Thorsteinsson,
2009). A similar approach has been adapted by studies of emotions in computing
and focusing on user experience (UX). By manipulating key elements graphically
such as by using colours and personiﬁcation, learning outcomes were found to
have improved signiﬁcantly (Haaranen, Ihantola, Sorva, & Vihavainen, 2015).
Hence, Uzun and Yıldırım (2018) advocate the hypothetical role of multimedia
elements in representing emotion and its beneﬁts to instructional designers and
educationists in developing learning contents.
   In tandem, one of the methods that has been applied to integrate emotions in
multimedia-based learning is the Theory of Emotional Design (Norman, 2004).
This theory, which is based on cognitive science theories (Pengnate & Sarathy,
2017) and product design, classiﬁes interaction with a product based on three
levels; visceral (aesthetic appeal), behavioural (usability) and reﬂective (reﬂective
thoughts) (Norman, 2004). Aesthetics which is the appreciation of beauty
(Maity, Madrosiya, & Bhattacharya, 2016) is basically used to “hook” learners
through good user interface that creates a positive learning environment by pro-
moting better information literacy and communication online (Porter, 2017). By
way of aesthetic manipulation, the emotional design could fulﬁl some of the
emotional and cognitive needs of the user (Triberti, Chirico, Rocca, & Riva,
2017). However, there has been limited research on how aesthetically pleasing
online learning environments inﬂuences learning outcomes (Porter, 2017). Sec-
ondly, even if emotional design has the potential to improve students’ online
                                     NEW REVIEW OF HYPERMEDIA AND MULTIMEDIA    59


learning performance (Schneider, Nebel, & Rey, 2016). Uzun and Yıldırım
(2018) advocate the importance of considering the pros and cons of using
emotional design in multimedia-based learning for each context.
   The eﬀects of emotional design and aesthetics are not the only two com-
ponents that aﬀect cognitive and aﬀective learning outcomes. Sometimes
designers tend to assume that a single design could appeal to all users and
this is contradicting as the attractiveness of something is subjective and so is
the users’ appraisal. Then again, aesthetics alone is not suﬃcient in determining
learning outcomes as users have discrete digital experiences (Grushka & Bellette,
2016) that reﬂect as to how they psychological relate aesthetics with multimedia
elements (Soleymani, Yang, Irie, & Hanjalic, 2015). Hence, it is also important
for instructional designers to take into account user characteristics in the
hopes of designing more personally satisfying and motivating learning environ-
ments (Uzun & Yıldırım, 2018). Consequently, these factors have called for
understanding meta-cognition of cognitive and aﬀective processing (Stark,
Brünken, & Park, 2018) which links emotions in multimedia (Leutner, 2014)
and concurrently was highlighted in the Cognitive Aﬀective Theory of Learning
with Media (CATLM) (Moreno & Mayer, 2007).
   In this study, emotional intelligence (EI) was named as a metacognitive trait
that inﬂuences online learning. EI, which is a skill that caters upon understand-
ing and managing of emotions, is a trait that enables students to cope with nega-
tivity (Berenson, Boyles, & Weaver, 2008). These repertoires of emotional skills
have been found to be important in learning, professional growth, and everyday
decisions. For engineering graduates, EI is fundamental in securing employment
and career growth (Riemer, 2003) as it relates to conﬁdence, motivation, self-dis-
cipline, self-regulation and leadership skills (Zeidner, Matthews, & Roberts,
2009). In terms of technology, EI has been found to helps people adapt to
new technology (Rocha & Santos, 2014) and has been found to have a positive
impact in e-learning (Arockiam & Selvaraj, 2011). EI enables people to manage
their emotion for a positive outcome and according to Han and Johnson (2012)
there is a need to explore how emotions, EI and e-learning are inter-related
especially in technology-based teaching and learning. Therefore, this study
would explore how cogitative and aﬀective learning outcomes, such as achieve-
ment, motivation and satisfaction, are inﬂuenced by emotional design when EI is
taken into account. Furthermore, instead of investigating diﬀerent disciplines
using a general topic-based courseware, this study will speciﬁcally explore
how emotional design aﬀects engineering undergraduates based on a course
taken by them.


2. Literature review
In HCI, user interaction has been widely studied based on cognitive outcomes
but scarcely in regard to the role of emotions (Yan et al., 2008). However,
60      J. A. KUMAR ET AL.


studies have demonstrated a signiﬁcant relationship between HCI and emotions
(Gratch, Marsella, & Petta, 2009) and have called for exploring this relationship
to better understand users’ experience online (Harper, Rodden, Rogers, & Sellen,
2008). There are three prime areas of emotional research in computing; aﬀective
computing, aﬀective interaction and technology as experience (Höök, 2013).
This study emphasises on “technology as experience” as it focuses on design
issues related to aesthetics and users’ experience. Theoretically, emotions
could be induced based on the attractiveness of the visual design (Um, Plass,
Hayward, & Homer, 2011) and by careful selection of aﬀective quality
(emotional value) of the multimedia elements (Knautz, 2012; Zhang, 2007).
Similarly, emotional design (Norman, 2004) also describes aesthetics (visceral)
as crucial in inﬂuencing cognition, perception and intend of using a product.
As aesthetics is the initial experience, the adoption of a product and the success-
ful implementation of it depends heavily on how the users are intrigue and later
satisﬁed with their interaction. However, what is beautiful is subjective, thus
designing for it is also subjective. Altogether, designing for aesthetics is also
designing for emotions (Idler, 2012) and it is achievable by the harmonious
use of colours (Höök, 2013), Gestalt eﬀects (Sutcliﬀe, 2009), aﬀective images,
animation, audio, video, and also through text/font (Dong, 2007, 2010). Accord-
ing to Porter (2017), the use of aesthetics in education is not about changing the
contents, designing new type of art or replacing core instructional design models
like ADDIE, but to be a complimentary aspect to the learning experience that
creates engaging interaction.
   For that purpose, we need to explore user experience and how users relate and
perceive a learning tool. Therefore, learning tools are perceived as a product and
not solely a tool that is used to transfer contents to the users. Emotional design
provides a model that assimilates interesting and relevant elements that motivate
learning (Uzun & Yıldırım, 2018) and it is based on product design theories that
highlight the same emotional connections such as understanding satisfaction
and motivation. Consequently, this also means that educators and designers
are faced by a challenge that requires the replication emotional interactions
between a teacher and a student such as engagement, motivation and satisfaction
to be also imitated in an online learning platform (Nurminen, Leppänen,
Väätäjä, & Ihantola, 2017).


2.1. Designing emotion for multimedia learning

One of the methods commonly used for designing emotions in online learning is
by applying seductive multimedia details to create engagement. However,
studies have shown that these seductive details may not have actually enhanced
the learning experience (Uzun & Yıldırım, 2018) as there is no harmonisation
with cognitive theories of learning (Heidig, Müller, & Reichelt, 2015; Mayer &
Estrella, 2014). Based on prior studies on emotional design in multimedia
                                      NEW REVIEW OF HYPERMEDIA AND MULTIMEDIA     61


learning, the focus has been on designing for positive emotions which are
labelled as positive designs and comparing it to neutral designs. Multimedia
elements that reﬂect positive emotions, such as joy and happiness, are character-
ised by using bright warm colours (Dong, 2010; Plass, Heidig, Hayward, Homer,
& Um, 2013), speciﬁc fonts (Shaikh, Chaparro, & Fox, 2006), positive aﬀective
images and videos (Knautz, Neal, Schmidt, Siebenlist, & Stock, 2011). A
neutral design, on the other hand, is deﬁned as a design that is neither positive
nor negative (Plass et al., 2013). Nevertheless, design that is deﬁned as “negative”
is understudied and we question the eﬀects of such negative designs in the learn-
ing environment. It was theorised that to design for negative emotions is to
design based on the representations of negative emotions such as sadness and
anger and concurrently the opposite of positive design. In addition, designing
for negative emotions might sound ridiculous and against design principles.
Nevertheless, just to assert, negative design does not mean negative usability.
Here we recognise negative design here as the opposite of positive design.
Thus, when positive emotions are designed to portray happiness, the opposite
that is by applying dark colours that theoretically portray sadness. Nonetheless,
we questioned if dark colours were actually detrimental to learning and if the
preference may be relevant to individual characteristics. Additionally, negative
emotions have also been found to be beneﬁcial to learning (Knautz et al.,
2011; Um et al., 2011). Hence, a research gap was apparent in exploring negative
emotions in design (Dong, 2007; Stark et al., 2018) similar to the eﬀects of nega-
tive visual aesthetics (Tractinsky, 2013).
   Next, according to Mayer and Estrella (2014) and Plass, Homer, and Kinzer
(2014), one of the main roles of emotional design in multimedia learning is to
further investigate how it relates to the Cognitive and Aﬀective Theory of Learn-
ing with Media (CATLM). Multimedia-based models, such as The Integrated
Model of Multimedia Interactivity (INTERACT) (Domagk, Schwartz, & Plass,
2010) and Integrated Cognitive Aﬀective Model of Learning with Multimedia
(ICALM) (Plass & Kaplan, 2015), have highlighted the need to understand
the characteristics of learners based on metacognitive mediation and individual
diﬀerences. In this study, we explored the mediating role of emotional intelli-
gence (EI) on learning outcomes in emotional designed multimedia-based learn-
ing environment. This is in line with research focusing in understanding how
technology can help develop emotional intelligence (EI) (Furger, 2014;
Pappas, 2015) and how non-cognitive states (anxiety, openness and conscien-
tiousness) relate to the application of multimedia elements (Leutner, 2014) in
a learning tool.


2.2. Relevant studies on multimedia-induced emotion in e-learning systems
According to Uzun and Yıldırım (2018), research on emotional design in multi-
media learning mostly focused on using colours, anthropomorphism and baby-
62      J. A. KUMAR ET AL.


face bias which were designed to represent positive emotions which were then
compared to neutral designs. In reference to the studies in this area, the most
widely studied multimedia interface is based on the original study by Um
(2008) on “How immunization works”. It is a 7-minute multimedia-based
lesson that is designed to induce positive emotion based on warm colour and
anthropomorphism. The control variable, which is the neutral design, was
designed in grey-scale without anthropomorphism. It was found that positive
design was more beneﬁcial than neutral design in regard to learning achieve-
ment, intrinsic motivation and the overall perception of learning. Using the
same multimedia lesson with variation in colour and shape, Um et al. (2011)
found that an emotionally designed system inﬂuences learning outcome irre-
spective of mental eﬀort or motivation and does not increase cognitive load.
A similar study found that colour alone did not inﬂuence students’ emotion
(Plass et al., 2013) and emotional design only improved learning motivation
and not satisfaction nor learning achievement. Using the same design (colours
and anthropomorphism) with eye tracking technology, it was found that
emotional design (with anthropomorphism) and students’ emotional state
aﬀected learning outcomes cognitively and aﬀectively (Park, Knörzer, Plass, &
Brünken, 2015). In addition, anthropomorphism was found to have a positive
impact on engagement (eye tracking) but did not induce positive emotions
while learning. In the same study, it was also reported that aesthetics and usabil-
ity inﬂuenced learning outcomes and intrinsic motivation.
   In another study, Uzun and Yıldırım (2018) explored how emotional design
in multimedia learning eﬀected middle school students’ emotions, mental eﬀort
and learning achievement through the variation of four diﬀerent designs; colour-
ful, neutral, anthropomorphism, and anthropomorphism with sound. It was
found that more mental eﬀort was needed for the colourful design in compari-
son to all other designs. Secondly, there was no diﬀerence in regard to learning
transfer; however, students who interacted with the colourful design showed
better outcomes than students using the other designs. Haaranen et al. (2015),
on the other hand, found that in the context of applying emotional design in
programming learning contents, students who were exposed to anthropo-
morphic graphics spend less time on the learning material in comparison to
those exposed to abstract graphics. Respondents exposed to anthropomorphic
graphics found the learning material as less intelligible and attractive, but no
diﬀerences in comprehension were observed between both groups. Mayer and
Estrella (2014) on the other hand investigated the eﬀects of emotionally designed
multimedia learning system titled “How Cold Virus Attacks the Body” and
found that emotional design improves comprehension, motivates students to
put in more eﬀort, and was found to beneﬁcial if there is no time limit. Dong
(2007) reported that by using a 4-minute multimedia learning system title
“How lightning forms” found that aesthetically designed system (positive) had
a positive eﬀect on user emotions compared to the neutral designs. It was
                                     NEW REVIEW OF HYPERMEDIA AND MULTIMEDIA     63


reported that the positive design improved problem-solving skills and both
designs did not aﬀect recall test. Heidig et al. (2015) investigated how aesthetics
(classical vs. expressive aesthetics) inﬂuenced learning outcome and found lear-
ner’s emotional state had a minor eﬀect on learning outcomes but had a large
impact on student’s intrinsic motivation.
   According to Lewis, Dontcheva, and Gerber (2011), positive and negative
images have a positive inﬂuence on creativity. It was observed that positive
(joy) and certain negative emotions (fear and anger) induced through multime-
dia elements (music, voice tone and avatar) promoted learning comprehension
compared to neutral systems for the primary school students (Ghali & Frasson,
2010). A study done by Stark et al. (2018) found that multimedia instructions
(written text with static picture) representing positive and negative emotional
text had better learning outcomes than the neutral one (control group).
Hence, both positive and negative emotional designs were beneﬁcial in improv-
ing learning outcomes through cognitive processing. According to Uzun and
Yıldırım (2018), emotional design in multimedia improves the perception of
easiness of a task therefore inﬂuential in motivating students in putting in eﬀort.
   Therefore, to conclude, it is clear that the outcomes relating to emotional
design in multimedia have varied based on diﬀerent demographics. According
to Haaranen et al. (2015), the eﬀect of emotional design in online learning
which depends on the combination of diﬀerent visualising aspects may not
apply for all types of contents and users. Hence we stipulate that the application
of emotional design strategies should be speciﬁc to users’ characteristics and
individuality. We also observed that this is similar to the individuality aspect
that relates to metacognition (CATLM). In regard to emotional design studies
in Malaysia nor in engineering education, we have not found, to date, any
related studies that have explored the potential beneﬁts of emotional design in
multimedia-based learning in these contexts.


2.3. Designing multimedia-based engineering education in Malaysia
Interactive multimedia is an excellent tool in engineering education because it
provides a unique method for teaching and learning engineering concepts
(Mohler, 2001) which is economical (Youn, Chyung, Moll, & Berg, 2010). By inte-
grating simulations and multimedia, engineering students would be able to better
understand engineering subjects (Delale, Liaw, Jiji, Voiculescu, & Yu, 2011). In
addition, by visually representing information, students are subjected to diverse
learning methods that enable them to understand diﬃcult contents. Undergradu-
ates in Malaysian higher learning institutions encourage the idea of applying mul-
timedia learning environment so that it supports the traditional classroom
teaching (Rassiah, Chidambaram, & Sihombing, 2011) as per a blended learning
environment. Nonetheless, designing for Malaysians may not be similar to the
design requirements of western countries. The rich culture of the country and
64      J. A. KUMAR ET AL.


preference of aesthetics may vary from the western culture. However, theoreti-
cally we reckoned that it may be absurd to design based on race and choose to
exempt design elements that were sensitive towards certain races.
   Next, e-learning users are currently emphasising aesthetics to inﬂuence user
experience (UX) (Stenalt & Godsk, 2006) and even in HCI, cultural factors, such
as language, images, colour and layout, are integrated into a website design for
the purpose of creating user connection (Kang & Kovacevic, 2012). In the case of
engineering undergraduates, a study done by Saleeb and Dafoulas (2011) contra-
dicted the ﬁnding of Um, Song, and Plass (2007) where they found engineering
student perceived warm colours negatively. It has been reported by Kirkham,
Farkas, and Lidstrom (2006) that engineering students being mildly introverts
might prefer darker shades (blue-grey combination) or cold colours. Therefore
this will be reﬂected in the suggested design attributes for a negative design.


2.4. Emotional intelligence (EI) and e-learning

EI is deﬁned as the “the ability to monitor one’s own and others’ feelings and
emotions, to discriminate among them and to use this information to guide
one’s thinking and actions” (Salovey & Mayer, 1990, p. 189). EI relates to mana-
ging emotion (self and others) (Goleman, 1995) and it is a skill that uses emotion
to enhance cognition (Mayer, Roberts, & Barsade, 2008). The emotional intelli-
gence quotient (EQ) can be measured based on two methods; trait EI and ability
EI (Petrides, 2011). Trait EI is based on self-perception on how a person judges
their own emotions or others through a self-report, whereas ability EI concerns
with emotions that are tested cognitively through the performance test. Trait EI
was reported to achieve better success in measuring EI compared to ability EI
(Pérez, Petrides, & Furnham, 2005) especially in academic and scholastic per-
formance (Andrei & Petrides, 2013). Theoretically, students with high EI are
able to manage their emotion by regulating and converting negative emotions
so that it is advantageous to their academic achievement (Azlina & Lee, 2012)
and it is concluded that EI is inﬂuential in learning (cognitively and emotionally)
and has a signiﬁcant impact on personality development. Empirical studies by
Chisholm (2010), Noriah, Siti Rahayah, Izham, and Salleh (2009), Saibani,
Sabtu, Muhamad, Abd. Wahab, and Sahari (2013) have found that high EI
engineering graduates will be more desirable as they would reﬂect good leader-
ship and managerial skills and EI improvement will support engineering stu-
dent’s knowledge and skill acquisitions.
   According to Pakprod and Wannapiroon (2013), e-learning can contribute to
the development of EI. Empirical ﬁndings, such as by Behnke and Greenan
(2011), Berenson et al. (2008), Ivanović et al. (2014) and Rocha and Santos
(2014), have highlighted the need to investigate the role of EI in online learning
and the inﬂuence of EI on multimedia perception. Ciarrochi, Chan, and Caputi
(2000) claimed that high EI people have better accuracy in detecting emotions in
                                     NEW REVIEW OF HYPERMEDIA AND MULTIMEDIA    65


sounds and colours. They added that EI is not aﬀected by personality traits such
as neuroticism and openness to aesthetics. However, this ﬁnding was contra-
dicted by Carr (2011) that claims EI improves acceptability to aesthetics and
new experiences. Mayer, DiPaola, and Salovey (1990) added that EI is beneﬁcial
in aesthetics from two aspects; (a) the evaluation of a visual stimuli especially
when it is related to a negative emotional experience, and (b) the ability to per-
ceive the right emotions from multimedia aesthetics which may contradict with
their cultural background. The ability of high EI individuals in “managing”
negative emotions establishes that this trait is beneﬁcial in long-term memory
processing as they are all able to recall negative stories that was induced (Ciar-
rochi, Chan, & Bajgar, 2001). In addition, they are also more capable of emotion-
ally designing projects by integrating emotions and aesthetics (Mayer & Salovey,
1997). EI is claimed to be an inﬂuencing factor in the ability of an individual to
perceive aesthetics especially in the neuropsychological studies (Cupchik & La
szlo, 1992). Studies relevant to cognition and emotion have led to the develop-
ment of a new ﬁeld of study called Neuroaesthetics that investigates how aes-
thetics and neuroscience relate (Nami & Ashayeri, 2011). EI is seen as
important trait that moderates how users adapt and perceive their environment,
process information and relate to technology innovation (Ivanović et al., 2014)


2.5. Designing multimedia for diﬀerent levels of emotional intelligence
EI skills are being integrated into various kinds of technology applications
(Ivonin, Chang, Chen, & Rauterberg, 2012) including e-learning (Lendi,
2011). It is a signiﬁcant skill that inﬂuences cognitive achievement even
though debatable by some scholars. The importance of EI is about adaptability.
Emotional control and management in learning help create a balance individual
which substantially is important as a twenty-ﬁrst century skill. Learning also
requires emotional management (Darling-Hammond et al., 2003) and EI is
one of the solutions in managing emotions related to learning (Aminuddin,
Tajularipin, & Rohaizan, 2009; Caust, 2008). In regard to design issues, (Ciarro-
chi et al., 2001) asserted that high EI people have better accuracy in detecting
emotions in sounds and colours and were not aﬀected by personality traits
such as neuroticism and openness to aesthetics. However, this contradicted
(Carr, 2011) that claims EI does improve openness to aesthetics and new experi-
ences and hence plays a signiﬁcant role in adaptability. According to Lea,
Qualter, Davis, Pérez-González, and Bangee (2018), HEI individuals were
more attentive to positive emotional stimuli in comparison to negative or
neutral ones. EI has also been claimed to be beneﬁcial in aesthetic perceptions
based on the assessment of a visual stimulus especially relating to negative
emotional experience, and the ability to perceive the right emotions from multi-
media aesthetics which may contradict with their cultural background (Mayer
et al., 1990).

3. Research questions and hypotheses
This study seeks to contribute to the understanding of the relationship between
emotional design in multimedia learning and emotional intelligence towards
engineering undergraduates. Firstly, it is obvious that research in emotional
design in multimedia learning so far has focused on certain western countries.
Thus, there has been a gap on how it inﬂuences other cultures especially in
Asian countries. Secondly, the respondents in previous studies have been
mostly from non-technical background and the topic covered has been on
very abstract subject which were non-relevant to their course. Studies have
also focused on comparing the eﬀect of positive design against neutral design
and thus neglected the eﬀect of negative design. Next, there is insuﬃcient litera-
ture on the eﬀects of EI towards aesthetics and online learning. In addition,
always the topic selected has been a general topic and not speciﬁc to a line of
learning discipline. Therefore, we decided to adopt previous methodology
used by Dong (2010), Mayer and Estrella (2014) and Um et al. (2011) and
add the construct of negative design and EI to explore learning outcomes on a
speciﬁc discipline of respondents (engineering). Based on the literature and pre-
vious ﬁndings, the below research questions were formulated.

(1) What are the diﬀerences in learning outcomes when diﬀerent types of
    emotionally designed multimedia learning system are used?
(2) What is the role of EI in emotional design towards the learning outcomes?
(3) What is the role of EI in achieving learning outcomes in an e-learning
    system?

   The learning outcomes measured in this study are learning achievement
(cognitive assessment), perceived intrinsic motivation and perceived satisfac-
tion (emotional assessment). As emotion and cognition go hand in hand
(Norman, 2004), it was essential to have both measurements. The hypoth-
eses were selected as null hypotheses as there is of no account on relevant
studies for the sample (Malaysian) relating to emotional design and
emotional intelligence in multimedia-based e-learning. The hypotheses for
this study are:
     Hypothesis 1: There are no diﬀerences between positive, neutral and negative emotion-
     ally designed multimedia learning system on learning achievement, perceived intrinsic
     motivation and satisfaction.

     Hypothesis 2: There are no diﬀerences between students with high or low emotional
     intelligence when exposed to multimedia learning system on learning achievement,
     perceived intrinsic motivation and satisfaction.

     Hypothesis 3: There are no interaction eﬀects between emotional design and emotional
     intelligence on learning achievement, perceived intrinsic motivation and satisfaction.

4.1. Approach
The approach proposed is by designing the multimedia learning system (course-
ware) to induce positive (happy), negative (sad) and neutral emotions through
multimedia elements (colour, images and font). The neutral version is regarded
as the control group as done by all three courseware have similar narration
(neutral voice), navigation, video and animations. Table 1 describes the contex-

(PosD) was used for evaluating if the contents and design were suitable for
the sample. Students, subject matter experts, language experts and instructional
design experts assessed the suitability of the courseware prior to the intervention.
Adjustments were made to the design to fulﬁl the suggestion of the experts prior
to designing the Neutral Design (NeuD) and the Negative Design (NegD).

4.3. Research design

Research design is a procedure in research that involves data collection, data
analysis and outcome reporting (Creswell, 2012). This study was designed as a
quantitative experimental research. Experimental research works are studies
where variables or conditions are manipulated by the researcher for a selected
number of research participants (Neuman, 2011). This study is based on a
                                      NEW REVIEW OF HYPERMEDIA AND MULTIMEDIA     69


3 × 2 factorial design and was design-based quasi-experimental design. Quasi-
experiment was used as intact classes were used for each treatment (PosD,
NeuD and NegD) and there was no random assignment of respondent to
each treatment. In addition, to ensure the internal and external validity,
threats such as selection bias, Hawthorne eﬀect and diﬀusion treatment (Cres-
well, 2012) were eliminated through these procedures.


4.4. Questionnaires

The learning achievement test was derived based on the diﬀerence between the
pre- and posttest (Ipek, 2010). It was developed as a 20-item multiple-choice
objective question test and was validated by three content experts. Both tests
had similar questions and answer items (Youn et al., 2010) but varied in
sequence and answer orientation (Revels, 2013). The test was validated by
content experts and had small Standard Error of Measurement (SEM = 2.014)
which reﬂected the accuracy and reliability of the test (Tighe, McManus,
Dewhurst, Chis, & Mucklow, 2010). The perceived intrinsic motivation was
measured based on the Post-Experimental Intrinsic Motivation Inventory
(IMI) (Deci & Ryan, 1985) grounded on the Activity Perception Questionnaire.
The questionnaire had a Cronbach alpha reliability of .883. Perceived satisfac-
tion was explored based on E-Learning Satisfaction (ELS) Inventory developed
by (Wang, 2003) and had Cronbach alpha value resulting to .888.
    Finally, students were categorised based on their EI levels that were evaluated
using the Traits Emotional Intelligence Questionnaire – Short Form (TEIQue–
SF) (Petrides & Furnham, 2006). The questionnaire had a Cronbach alpha value
of .778. Respondents were grouped based on high and low levels of EI that were
determined by the mean value (Behnke & Greenan, 2011). Even though some
scholars are against such segregation especially for EI, the researchers executed
this method to relate to discussion relevant to the literature. In addition, a quan-
titative method, which was used to obtain the data (motivation, satisfaction and
EI) according to (Rienties & Rivers, 2014), has shown that it is a much more
appropriate method in gaining emotional data in regard to e-learning. All ques-
tionnaires applied the 7-point Likert scale ranging from one (strongly disagree)
to seven (strongly agree).


5. Research procedures
The study was executed in computer laboratories where each student was
assigned a desktop computer to execute the courseware. Every institute was
assigned to one type of courseware; PosD, NeuD or NegD. The total time for
intervention is 180 min (3 h) which was based on the syllabus requirement
for one week of interaction time. The intervention was based on three stages
(pre-intervention, intervention and post-intervention). The pre-intervention
70     J. A. KUMAR ET AL.


included administrating the pretest (30 min) and exploring students’ emotional
intelligence through TEIQue–SF (20 min). Pretest sensitisation was minimised
by concealing the purpose of the pretest (Zygmont & Smith, 2014) and by not
exposing the answers to the instructors or students (Quitadamo & Kurtz,
2007). The second stage (intervention) requires students to go through the
manual and the multimedia learning system (45 min). Extra time was given
for students to go through the system until the hour mark was over. As the cour-
seware only required 45 min, the additional 15 min allowed the students to
explore the courseware further without the stress of time limit. According to
Mayer and Estrella (2014), their empirical ﬁndings found that emotional
design improves learning outcomes if there is no time limit. However, in this
context as the design of the courseware was based on speciﬁc topic and is in
accordance with the student learning time (SLT) from the syllabus, the interven-
tion had to ﬁt into the given time frame and to ensure there was suﬃcient time to
implement the post-intervention. As for the last stage that is the post-interven-
tion, students were required to provide feedback based on the IMI (15 min) and
ELS (15 min) questionnaires prior to the posttest (30 min).

7. Discussion
Therefore based on the ﬁrst research question; does emotionally designed multi-
media learning system eﬀect learning outcomes? It was found that emotionally
designed system (PosD and NegD) improved learning achievement in compari-
son to the NeuD system. Similar outcome was also reported based on studies
                                        NEW REVIEW OF HYPERMEDIA AND MULTIMEDIA        73

done on an e-learning system EWLearning (Ghali & Frasson, 2010) and emo-
tionality in text (Stark et al., 2018), where both positive and negative designs
had beneﬁcial eﬀects on achievement. However, this also contradicts the
ﬁndings from other studies (Chaﬀar & Frasson, 2005; Khairudin, Givi, Wan
Shahrazad, Nasir, & Halim, 2011) that found negative designs as detrimental.
Studies focusing on comparing positive and neutral design (Dong, 2007;
Mayer & Estrella, 2014; Park et al., 2015) also reported signiﬁcant diﬀerence
in learning achievement between these two designs (positive and neutral);
however, no comparison was done on the eﬀects of negative designs. Thus,
this motivated this study. Comparing a colourful design towards a neutral one
disregards the nature of today’s technology savvy users who are constantly
exposed to aesthetics through user graphical interaction. Neutral designs
which apply greyscale colours have been found to be demotivating in online
learning and have a negative eﬀect on recall test and learning achievement
(Uzun & Yıldırım, 2018). However, there was improvement in learning achieve-
ment for the neutral design and we reasoned this outcome as an eﬀect of using a
computer-based learning medium in contrast to their traditional learning
environment.
   Conversely, the ﬁndings of this study suggest that negative and positive
designs share similar beneﬁts on learning achievement even if the respondents
performed slightly better in the negative design (not signiﬁcant). We believe
that this is attributed towards engineering students’ introvert personality that
has been linked to preference of the outer space-technical look as portrayed in
the negative design (Kirkham et al., 2006). Similar colour preferences for e-
learning interface were also reported by Saleeb and Dafoulas (2011) in a study
done on designing 3D virtual learning space for engineering students.
   Secondly, the emotional design encourages students to put in more eﬀort in
learning (Um et al., 2011) as it inﬂuences generative processing (Mayer &
Estrella, 2014). This is related to the cognition aspect of learning. In this
study, signiﬁcant diﬀerences between designs were only found on learning
achievement (cognitive) and not for perceived satisfaction and intrinsic motiv-
ation (aﬀective). Similarly, Plass et al. (2013) and Um et al. (2011) reported sig-
niﬁcant diﬀerence in learning achievement and not in motivation when they
74      J. A. KUMAR ET AL.


compared positive and neutral designs. We hypothesised that the novelty of the
courseware irrespective of the design type was a vast upgrade from the tra-
ditional PowerPoint slides students are accustomed to. The courseware also
may have catered to their independent learning style and choice of discipline
(Jailani, Wan Mohd Rashid, & Ahmad Rizal, 2007), hence a gain in learning
achievement. Therefore, Hypothesis 1 was rejected as there were signiﬁcant
diﬀerences between emotionally designed multimedia courseware (positive
and negative) with the neutral design.
   For the second research question; does emotional intelligence (EI) inﬂuence
learning outcomes when students are exposed to multimedia learning system?
In this study, it can be concluded that as per how emotional design inﬂuences
the cognitive aspect of learning, emotional intelligence aﬀects the emotional
aspect which is the intrinsic motivation and satisfaction. High eﬀect size
obtained for both these outcomes conveyed that irrespective of the design
type, EI inﬂuences student’s intention of using a learning system and their
level of satisfaction. Prior studies claim that EI inﬂuences how users adapt to
e-learning emotionally (Behnke & Greenan, 2011) but has little impact on
their learning achievement (Lendi, 2011). EI has been found to also improve aca-
demic achievement as it inﬂuences the emotions related to handling educational
stress (Fayombo, 2012; Park & Lim, 2007; Stamatopoulou, Kargakou, Konstan-
tarogianni, & Prezerakos, 2015); however, it does not mediate their learning
achievement for a speciﬁc task, activity or course (Lee, Park, Ryu, Oh, & Jan,
2012). Overall, an above average mean score for both levels of EI showed that
both groups (HEI and LEI) perceived that they have improved after using the
learning tool (Eow, Wan Zah, Roselan, & Rosnaini, 2010; Youn et al., 2010).
We reasoned the signiﬁcant diﬀerences found between both groups as due to
the characters of HEI students having low computer anxiety (Zare & Nouri,
2013) and were reported to generally be more satisﬁed with e-learning tools
in comparison to LEI students whom prefer traditional classroom setting
(Kang, Kim, & Chong, 2011). Therefore, Hypothesis 2 was also rejected as sig-
niﬁcant diﬀerences were found for emotional learning outcomes (intrinsic
motivation and satisfaction) but not for cognitive learning outcome (learning
achievement) in regard to diﬀerent levels of EI.
   Finally, for the third research question, is there an interaction eﬀect between
emotional design and EI on the learning outcomes? In this study, the only inter-
action eﬀect found between emotional design and EI is based on perceived sat-
isfaction. HEI students tend to favour designs that were emotionally designed,
whereas LEI students showed preference for neutral designs. As more mental
eﬀort is needed for emotional design (Mayer & Estrella, 2014), it suits the think-
ing style and creativity of HEI students (Murphy & Janeke, 2009; Olatoye, Akin-
tunde, & Yakasai, 2010) thus higher levels of satisfaction. Secondly, aesthetics are
also perceived as stimulating for HEI individuals (Pappas, 2015) and they are
able to identify precisely with emotions portrayed by multimedia elements
                                      NEW REVIEW OF HYPERMEDIA AND MULTIMEDIA     75


(Brackett, Rivers, & Salovey, 2011). LEI students, on the other hand, are not very
acceptable towards aesthetics (Carr, 2011) therefore identifying better with
neutral design. Therefore, Hypothesis 3 was also rejected as signiﬁcant diﬀer-
ences were found between satisfaction, emotional design and EI and not for
the other variables.


8. Empirical, theoretical and practical contributions
The contribution of this study will be discussed based on empirical, theoretical
and practical contributions. The empirical contribution of this study showed
that positive and negative emotional induction in a MLS had similar eﬀect on
learning. Both of these design aesthetics through emotional design improved
learning achievement; however, it did not aﬀect learner intrinsic motivation
and satisfaction on its own. The better predictor of aﬀective outcomes in e-learn-
ing was determined by EI of the students and not solely on the design aspects.
Related to this, satisfaction in using an emotionally designed system does not
just depend on the “attractiveness” of a design, but also on how the respondents
emotionally manage this “attractiveness”. Nevertheless, the ﬁndings support the
discovery by Mayer and Estrella (2014), Plass et al. (2013), Um et al. (2011) that
emotional design is beneﬁcial in e-learning cognitively. The major empirical
ﬁndings engaged the current research from Western countries to Malaysia
which focused on polytechnic students. In addition, as studies in this ﬁeld con-
centrated on biology (“How immunization works”) or environment-based (How
lightning forms) short e-learning materials, this study used engineering
materials (Silicon Formation) and disregarded the use of anthropomorphism
to produce the same ﬁndings. Therefore, the need of using anthropomorphism
in inducing emotions in e-learning such as by Heidig et al. (2015) and Plass et al.
(2013) is questioned. The use of anthropomorphism in emotional design will
cause researchers to lose valuable research ﬁnding especially when negative aes-
thetics are investigated. In addition, this study contributed to the role of EI when
dealing with aesthetics in e-learning which are still in the early stages.
   One of the main reasons cognitive theory of multimedia learning (CTML)
was “expanded” to cognitive-aﬀective theory of learning with media
(CATLM) is because Moreno and Mayer (2007) found that there are other moti-
vational and meta-cognitive factors that inﬂuence learning outcome cognitively.
The “attractiveness” of a lesson (emotionally designed MLS) is a motivating
factor that inﬂuences engagement and also cognitive processing (Leutner,
2014; Mayer & Estrella, 2014; Plass et al., 2013). This is also parallel with the
understanding on the theory of emotional design in multimedia learning,
where the attractiveness of the design is not attributed to additional, non-rel-
evant elements but through redesigned contents that will engage/motivate the
user which simultaneously lead to satisfaction. Nevertheless, the CATLM also
highlighted meta-cognitive factors, which in this study is incorporated
76      J. A. KUMAR ET AL.


through emotional intelligence. Meta-cognitive is deﬁned as “the ability to
monitor mental states and to control them adaptively” (Zeidner et al., 2009,
p. 157) sounds similar to EI as to control one’s mental state will also require
emotional management. Therefore, based on this understanding it could be
deduced that motivational factors (emotional design) in CATLM might have a
shorter eﬀect (working memory) where the results might be apparent through
achievement test such as retention, comprehension and recall test. However,
the meta-cognitive (EI) aﬀect inﬂuenced aﬀective outcome relating to long-
term memory that improved their overall learning as it betters cognitive abilities
to adapt to changes. In addition, these meta-cognitive abilities are the skills that
would improve overall academic achievements which inﬂuenced learning
achievement. It also contributed to the understanding of non-cognitive traits
such as EI as a moderating eﬀect on multimedia-based learning.
   Therefore, this study conforms with the ﬁndings of Mayer and Estrella (2014)
that multimedia teaching and learning will only be beneﬁcial if motivational
(emotional design) and aﬀective aspects (EI) are taken into account to ensure
positive cognitive and aﬀective learning outcomes. However, as highlighted by
Domagk et al. (2010) in their Integrated Model of Multimedia Interactivity
(INTERACT), there is an interconnection between cognition, motivation and
emotion that is bidirectional and not unidirectional such as in CATLM. A
system should be designed to include these characteristics as they inter-relate
with each other namely; (i) learning environment (instructional design and
design standard), (ii) behavioural activities (physical interactions with the
system), (iii) cognitive and metacognitive activities (learning outcomes), (iv)
motivation and emotions, (v) learner characteristics and their (vi) mental
models (the knowledge prior and post the intervention) (Domagk et al.,
2010). In this study, the researcher suggests adding the bidirectional approach
of INTERACT in emotional design for multimedia learning especially for the
learner characteristics. The learner characteristics for instance which could
also be establish by the EI of the student is bidirectional where it inﬂuences e-
learning and vice versa; the success in an online assessment will motivate
users to engage in the system and high EI, for instance, will similarly motivate
engagement. This is also parallel with the ﬁndings of Park et al. (2015) and Inte-
grated Cognitive Aﬀective Model of Learning with Multimedia (ICALM) by
Plass and Kaplan (2015) where they concluded that emotional design in multi-
media learning should integrate both attributes of cognitive and aﬀective proces-
sing to support learning that is through understanding learner’s emotional state.
However, to just emphasise on the learner’s emotional state prior to interven-
tion, emotional design and EI should be integrated into multimedia learning
to foster better learning outcomes.
   The practical contribution of the study helps to better understand the need to
emotionally design MLS for polytechnic engineering students. However, as stu-
dents performed the best in NegD design, it should be highlighted that in any
                                      NEW REVIEW OF HYPERMEDIA AND MULTIMEDIA     77


case the design should be “suitable” with the personality of the intended users. The
slightly introvert personality of an engineering student has favoured darker more
techno design reﬂected by the negative design. Designer/lecturers should break the
common stereotype designing mentality be more acceptable towards designing for
user experience. In this study, it was found that negative might not be so bad for
learning after all; negative is not normal, but also not wrong, just diﬀerent. With
regard to the duration of the intervention, this study has found that by increasing
the exposure from <10 to 45 min, the results relating to the eﬀectiveness of
emotional design cognitively were also replicated.
    In addition, EI has been found to be important for polytechnic students to
cope with the emotional demands (intrinsic motivation and satisfaction) of e-
learning. For instance, to rake the beneﬁts of aesthetics and emotional design
in multimedia-based learning, this study asserted that EI levels determine the
abilities to cope with openness towards aesthetics and change in learning.
Their inabilities of managing these emotional changes might reﬂect towards
other aspects of their education and personal skills. With the vast use of technol-
ogy in the classroom, EI has the potential to be developed through external inter-
ventions (Lee et al., 2012) which includes e-learning (Pappas, 2015). Therefore,
this study suggests that EI be integrated as part of the engineering education as it
will aid students coping mechanism cognitively, aﬀectively and socially consist-
ent with ICT requirement for twenty-ﬁrst century global skills and address the
Malaysian National Philosophy of Education.


9. Conclusion
This study concludes that emotional design is a beneﬁcial tool in improving stu-
dents’ learning outcomes; however, the learner individual characteristics rep-
resented as their emotional intelligence were also found to be crucial in
students’ adaption of design and technology. In addition, we also investigated
the potential beneﬁts of negative design and found that it has similar beneﬁts
with positive design. We support the ﬁndings by (Stark et al., 2018) as we also
observe that both positive and negative designs are more inﬂuential in learning
than a neutral design. But, integrating EI into this study has been an eye opener
on the inﬂuence of emotions in this context. EI has been found to be an impor-
tant aspect in adaption to ambiguous computing technology in e-learning and it
is undeniable when we talk about designing emotions we should consider the
impact of personal characteristic that inﬂuence emotions such as EI. We
observe that EI was a better predictor of emotional learning outcomes (intrinsic
motivation and satisfaction) than emotional design. However, we did ﬁnd an
interaction eﬀect between EI and emotional design for satisfaction which
reﬂected how diﬀerent levels were satisﬁed with each design type. We observed
that students with low levels of EI were more satisﬁed with neutral design in
comparison to emotional designed courseware. Therefore, we would also like

to add the importance of individuality and adaptability that are reﬂected through
EI as an important aspect to be explored in regard to online learning especially
through aesthetics.

10. Limitations and implication for future research
Some of the limitations of this study relate to the sample of the study, which
focuses only on electronic engineering students. It will be interesting to replicate
the ﬁndings of this study for a much more general subject and sample as the
inﬂuence of negative design is not widely studied. The results of this study
might have been partial towards NegD design due to the personality of engineer-
ing students, hence investigating the response for non-engineering subjects and
on non-technical subjects may provide additional knowledge on this context. In
addition, this study utilised multimedia elements that can be categorised based
on valance; nonetheless, auditory valence was not included in this study as the
respondents are not native English speakers. It will also be interesting to inves-
tigate how the individual and join eﬀects of colours and fonts eﬀected emotional
design on leaning. In addition, it can be suggested that future research studies
consider the use of neutral background colour with emotionally designed multi-
media elements (positive or negative) to investigate the same eﬀects. The neutral
background may be more accommodating for LEI students and emotionally
designed elements for satisfying HEI student’s aesthetic needs. Potentially,
mood induction may be explored to identify how it aﬀects learning outcomes
for students with diﬀerent levels of EI. (Triberti et al., 2017) suggested exploring
the eﬀects of emotional design by continuously measuring how certain emotions
may inﬂuence interaction. But, Schneider et al. (2016) suggested exploring the
eﬀects of decorative or seductive images to explore the same learning outcomes.
Finally, as emotional design caters to users’ satisfaction, (Heidig et al., 2015;
Raihan, 2017) also suggested exploring aesthetics perception in learning
environments’ user interface as there is scarce research in this ﬁeld.
